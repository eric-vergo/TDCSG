# The analytic radius of a two-disk system

Authors: Eric Vergo, Claude

**Statement.** Let $n = 5$ or $n \ge 7$. A function on the union of the two open disks of radius $r$
about $\mp 1$ that is invariant under the $2\pi/n$ rotation of each disk, and is real-analytic (or
harmonic, or holomorphic), is forced to be constant exactly when
$$
r \;>\; r_A(n) := \sec\frac{\pi}{n} \;=\; \frac{2}{|1+\zeta_n|}.
$$
At and below $r_A(n)$ non-constant holomorphic invariant functions exist. The threshold is the
radius at which the $n$ rotated copies of the lens close up, corner to corner, into a necklace
around each disk. It lies strictly below the touching radius $g(n) = |2-\zeta_n|$, with
$(g-1)/(r_A-1) \to 8$, and far below the critical radius $r_c(n)$ of the paper's table.

**Status.** The theorem (Theorem 4.1) is **[proved]** here, with complete elementary proofs. The proofs
cite three classical results: the Behnke–Stein theorem, Jordan's theorem on primitive groups and the
Steinitz lemma. The note has been independently refereed; the report is
[`referee-report-thread6.md`](referee-report-thread6.md), and §11 records its verdicts and what it
did not check. For $n=5$, $r_A<r_c$ is proved modulo the certified finiteness computation of
`critical-radius-n5.md` (refereed), and likewise for $n=8,10,12$ by the certificates of
[`../certificates.md`](../certificates.md) (§8). For the other $n$ it rests on the
paper's computed table of $r_c(n)$ and is **[computed]**. Small floating-point
computations confirm the proved statements (§10).

**What happens to the original idea.** The definition through the group generated by the
realized motions is trivial: that group is all of $\Gamma$ for every $r>0$. The hypothesis that
translation germs are absent for $r$ just above 1 is false: $a^{-1}b$ gives one for every $r>1$. The
idea that survives is a statement about domains. Below $r_A$ the pseudogroup of the two rotations is
already *full*: every germ is defined on a whole connected component of the set where it could be
defined. So the identity theorem adds nothing, and the quotient is an open Riemann surface. Above
$r_A$ the identity theorem enlarges one translation germ across a triple overlap. From then on
analytic continuation generates a dense set of translations, while the piecewise group $GG_n(r)$
stays finite up to $r_c(n)$. The claim $r_A < r_c$ holds. The threshold is $\sec(\pi/n)$, not $g(n)$.

## 0. Results

Labels follow [`../overview.md`](../overview.md).

1. **[proved]** (Theorem 4.1) For $n=5$ and $n\ge 7$, and for each of the classes real-analytic,
   harmonic and holomorphic, invariant functions on $\Omega_r = D_1\cup D_2$ (open disks) are all
   constant if and only if $r > \sec(\pi/n)$. For germs along the closed disks the condition is
   $r \ge \sec(\pi/n)$.
2. **[proved]** (Theorem 8.1, Corollary 8.2) The pseudogroup $P_r$ generated by the two rotations is
   full (closed under the identity theorem) for $r\le\sec(\pi/n)$. It is not full at any larger
   $r$ at which $GG_n(r)$ is finite. Its *analytic hull*
   $H_r$ realizes finitely many motions for $r \le \sec(\pi/n)$:
   - $2n^2-2n+1$ motions for odd $n$;
   - $2n^2-3n+3$ for even $n$.

   For $r>\sec(\pi/n)$ the hull contains translations by a set of vectors that is dense near 0, all
   acting on one fixed disk.
3. **[proved]** (Proposition 9.1) For all $r\in[1,\sec(\pi/n))$ the group $GG_n(r)$ has the same
   isomorphism type, of order $n^2(2n-1)!/2$. It is $C_n\times C_n\times A_{2n-1}$ for odd $n$, and an
   index-2 subgroup of $C_n\times C_n\times S_{2n-1}$ for even $n$. Every orbit has $1$, $n$ or
   $2n-1$ points there. For $r>\sec(\pi/n)$ some orbits have at least $3n-2$ points. So the **orbit
   structure** of $GG_n(r)$ first changes after $r=1$ exactly at $r_A$.

   Whether the abstract group also changes at $r_A$ is **open**. Floating-point computations for
   $n=5,7,8$ just above $r_A$ find induced permutation groups far larger than $n^2(2n-1)!/2$, but
   that is evidence, not proof.
4. **[proved]** $1<\sec(\pi/n)<g(n)$ for $n \ge 3$, and $\sec(\pi/n) = 1+\pi^2/(2n^2)+O(n^{-4})$
   while $g(n) = 1+4\pi^2/n^2+O(n^{-4})$.
5. **[computed]** Against the paper's table, $r_A < g \le r_c$ for $n=5,7,8,10,12$. The ratio
   $(r_c-1)/(r_A-1)$ is $4.87, 5.67, 8.63, 10.56, 10.67$.
6. **[proved]** (Corollary 8.3) On the whole interval $(r_A(n),r_c(n))$ the hull $H_r$ has orbits dense
   in open sets, while $GG_n(r)$ is finite and $P_r$ has only finite orbits. Here $r_c$ denotes the
   true critical radius.
   - The interval is non-empty for $n=5$ [proved modulo the certified computation of
     `critical-radius-n5.md`].
   - For the other $n$, non-emptiness rests on the paper's table [computed].

## 1. Setting and notation

- $\zeta=\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}$.
- $A(z)=\zeta^{-1}(z+1)-1$ and $B(z)=\zeta^{-1}(z-1)+1$ are the full-plane rotations by $-2\pi/n$
  about $-1$ and $+1$.
- $\Gamma=\langle A,B\rangle = T\rtimes\langle A\rangle$, with translation group
  $T=2(1-\zeta)\mathbb Z[\zeta]$. $T$ is dense in $\mathbb C$ for $n\notin\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.
- $D_1, D_2$ are the **open** disks of radius $r$ about $-1$ and $+1$; $\bar D_1,\bar D_2$ are the
  closed disks.
- $\Omega_r = D_1\cup D_2$, connected for $r>1$.
- $L = D_1\cap D_2$ is the open lens.
- $GG_n(r)=\langle a,b\rangle$, where $a = A$ on $\bar D_1$ and the identity elsewhere, and likewise
  for $b$.
- $t := 2(\zeta-1)$, so that $A^{-1}B(z) = z+t$.
- $g(n) = |2-\zeta| = \sqrt{5-4\cos(2\pi/n)}$.
- $r_c(n)$ is the critical radius of the paper.
- $\operatorname{mec}(p_1,\dots,p_k)$ is the radius of the smallest closed disk containing the
  points $p_i$.

**Lemma 1.1 (intersections of equal disks)** [proved]. Open disks of radius $r$ with centres
$c_1,\dots,c_k$ have a common point if and only if $\operatorname{mec}(c_1,\dots,c_k)<r$.

*Proof.* A point $y$ lies in all the disks iff $|y-c_i|<r$ for all $i$. If such a $y$ exists, the
closed disk of radius $\max_i|y-c_i|<r$ about $y$ contains every $c_i$. Conversely, the centre of
the minimal enclosing disk is within $\operatorname{mec}<r$ of every $c_i$. ∎

The minimal enclosing disk of three points is the circumcircle if the triangle is acute, and the
disk on the longest side otherwise. An isosceles triangle with legs $\ell$ and apex angle $\alpha$
has circumradius $\ell/(2\cos(\alpha/2))$.

**Lemma 1.2 (the necklace radius)** [proved]. Let $n\ge 5$ and $k\not\equiv 0 \pmod n$. Then
$$
\operatorname{mec}(-1,\,1,\,A^{k}(1)) \;\ge\; \sec(\pi/n),
$$
with equality exactly for $k\equiv\pm1$. The same holds for $\operatorname{mec}(1,-1,B^k(-1))$ and
for $\operatorname{mec}(-1,1,1-2\zeta)$, the last with equality.

*Proof.*
1. The triangle $(-1,1,A^k(1))$ is isosceles with apex $-1$, legs $2$ and apex angle $2\pi k'/n$,
   where $k'=\min(k\bmod n,\,n-k\bmod n)$.
2. If $2\pi k'/n<\pi/2$ the triangle is acute and the radius is $2/(2\cos(\pi k'/n))=\sec(\pi k'/n)$.
   This is at least $\sec(\pi/n)$, with equality iff $k'=1$.
3. If $2\pi k'/n \ge \pi/2$ the radius is half the base, $2\sin(\pi k'/n)\ge 2\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt2$.
   This exceeds $\sec(\pi/5)=1.236\ldots \ge \sec(\pi/n)$.
4. The $B$ case follows by the symmetry $z\mapsto -z$.
5. The triangle $(-1,1,1-2\zeta)$ has apex $1$, legs $|{-2}|=|{-2\zeta}|=2$ and apex angle $2\pi/n$.
   So its radius is $\sec(\pi/n)$. ∎

**Geometric meaning.** The upper corner of $L$ is $i\sqrt{r^2-1}$. Seen from $-1$ it has angle
$\arccos(1/r)$. So the $n$ rotated lenses $A^kL$ meet corner to corner exactly when
$\arccos(1/r)=\pi/n$, i.e. $r=\sec(\pi/n)$. At that radius $A(i\tan\frac{\pi}{n}) = -i\tan\frac{\pi}{n}$:
the rotation carries the upper corner to the lower one. For $r<\sec(\pi/n)$ the closed lenses
$A^k\bar L$ are pairwise disjoint; for $r>\sec(\pi/n)$ consecutive open lenses overlap. Lemma 1.2 says
this is the first time any three of the disks $D_1, D_2, \gamma D_j$ with $\gamma \in \{A^{\pm1},B^{\pm1}\}$
share a point.

## 2. Why the naive definitions are trivial

1. **The group generated by realized motions.** Let $\Gamma(r)\subset\Gamma$ be the set of motions
   realized by some element of $GG_n(r)$ on a piece with non-empty interior. It contains $A$ and
   $B$ (realized on $D_1$ and $D_2$). So $\langle\Gamma(r)\rangle=\Gamma$ for **every** $r>0$, and
   $\Gamma$ is dense. Non-discreteness of $\langle\Gamma(r)\rangle$ therefore gives the threshold $0$.
2. **Translation germs.** For every $r>1$, the word $a^{-1}b$ acts on the open set $B^{-1}(L)$ as the
   translation $z\mapsto z+t$. Indeed $z\in D_2$ and $Bz\in L\subset D_1$. Translation germs
   therefore exist as soon as the disks overlap. The hypothesis that "no word produces a translation
   germ for $r$ barely above 1" is false.
3. **A fixed large domain.** Suppose $f$ is real-analytic on a fixed connected open
   $U \supset \bar D_1\cup\bar D_2$, and is invariant on the disks. Then the identity theorem
   propagates $f\circ A=f$ to the component of $U\cap A^{-1}U$ containing $D_1$.
   - The answer depends on $U$. For $U=\mathbb C$, $f$ is $\Gamma$-invariant on $\mathbb C$, hence
     constant (for $n \notin \{2,3,4,6\}$) at every $r>0$.
   - The intrinsic choices are therefore the open union $\Omega_r$ itself, or germs along the closed
     disks (§3).

What matters is not which motions occur, but on which **domains** the identity theorem lets each
one act.

## 3. Definitions

**Definition 3.1 (invariant functions).** For a class
$\mathcal C\in\{\text{real-analytic},\ \text{harmonic},\ \text{holomorphic}\}$, let
$\mathcal F^{\mathcal C}_r$ be the set of $f\in\mathcal C(\Omega_r)$ with
$$
f\circ A=f \text{ on } D_1, \qquad f\circ B=f \text{ on } D_2 .
$$
Since $A$ maps $D_1$ onto itself, this also gives $f\circ A^k=f$ on $D_1$ for all $k\in\mathbb Z$, and
likewise for $B$.

Let $\bar{\mathcal F}^{\mathcal C}_r$ be the germ version. It consists of functions of class
$\mathcal C$ on some open neighbourhood of $\bar D_1\cup\bar D_2$ (depending on $f$), with
invariance on $\bar D_1$ and on $\bar D_2$.

**Definition 3.2 (analytic radius).**
$$
r_A^{\mathcal C}(n)=\inf\{\,r>1:\ \text{every } f\in\mathcal F_r^{\mathcal C} \text{ is constant}\,\}.
$$

**Lemma 3.3 (monotonicity)** [proved].
1. If $1<r<r'$ and $\mathcal F_{r'}^{\mathcal C}$ contains a non-constant function, so does
   $\mathcal F_r^{\mathcal C}$.
2. $\bar{\mathcal F}^{\mathcal C}_r$ contains a non-constant function if and only if
   $\mathcal F^{\mathcal C}_{r'}$ does for some $r'>r$.

*Proof.*
1. Restriction from $\Omega_{r'}$ to $\Omega_r$ preserves invariance. Since $\Omega_{r'}$ is
   connected, the identity theorem shows that the restriction is still non-constant.
2. For a germ, "non-constant" means non-constant on some neighbourhood of $\bar D_1\cup\bar D_2$. The
   domain $U$ may be disconnected, so constancy is always tested on a connected
   $\Omega_{r+\varepsilon}$.
3. For one direction, a function on $\Omega_{r'}$ is defined on a neighbourhood of
   $\bar D_1 \cup \bar D_2$.
4. For the other, let $f$ be defined on $U\supset\bar D_1\cup\bar D_2$. By compactness,
   $U\supset\Omega_{r+\varepsilon}$ for some $\varepsilon>0$; replace $f$ by its restriction there.
5. $f\circ A$ is defined on $A^{-1}(U)\supset D(-1,r+\varepsilon)$ and equals $f$ on $D(-1,r)$. So it
   equals $f$ on the connected set $D(-1,r+\varepsilon)$. The same holds for $B$. So
   $f|_{\Omega_{r+\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal F^{\mathcal C}_{r+\varepsilon}$. It is non-constant there,
   since otherwise it would be constant near $\bar D_1\cup\bar D_2$. ∎

So the set of radii with a non-constant invariant function is an interval $(1,r_A]$ or $(1,r_A)$.

**Definition 3.4 (pseudogroup, hull, fullness).**
1. **Domain of a word.** For a word $w=s_m\cdots s_1$ with $s_i\in\{A^{\pm1},B^{\pm1}\}$, let
   $\mathrm{Dom}(w)$ be the set of $z$ such that, for every $i$, the point $s_{i-1}\cdots s_1z$ lies
   in $D_1$ if $s_i=A^{\pm1}$ and in $D_2$ if $s_i=B^{\pm1}$.
   - $\mathrm{Dom}(w)$ is an intersection of open disks of radius $r$, hence convex.
   - On it, $w$ acts as the motion $\rho(w)\in\Gamma$.
2. **The pseudogroup $P_r$.** For $\gamma\in\Gamma$, let
   $\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma)=\bigcup\{\mathrm{Dom}(w): \rho(w)=\gamma\}$. The pseudogroup is
   $P_r=\{(\gamma,\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma))\}$.
   - This is the pseudogroup generated by $A|_{D_1}$ and $B|_{D_2}$.
   - Every $f\in\mathcal F^{\mathcal C}_r$ satisfies $f\circ\gamma=f$ on $\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma)$.
3. **Fullness.** A collection $\{(\gamma,U_\gamma)\}$ that is closed under composition and inverses
   is **full** if each $U_\gamma$ is a union of connected components of
   $\Omega_r\cap\gamma^{-1}\Omega_r$.
4. **The analytic hull $H_r$** is the smallest full collection containing $P_r$. It is reached by
   alternating two operations: closure under composition and inverses, and enlarging each domain to
   the union of the components it meets.
   - By the identity theorem, every invariant $f$ of any class satisfies $f\circ\gamma=f$ on
     $\mathrm{Dom}_H(\gamma)$.
   - Write $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)=\{\gamma:\mathrm{Dom}_H(\gamma)\neq\emptyset\}$.

$\Omega_r\cap\gamma^{-1}\Omega_r$ is the union of the four convex **atoms**
$D_i\cap\gamma^{-1}D_j$, for $i,j\in\{1,2\}$. Each component is a union of atoms, and two atoms lie
in one component iff they are linked by a chain of pairwise intersecting atoms. Each such
intersection is decided by Lemma 1.1.

## 4. The main theorem

**Theorem 4.1** [proved]. Let $n=5$ or $n\ge7$, and $r>1$. The following are equivalent:
1. $r\le\sec(\pi/n)$;
2. $\mathcal F^{\rm hol}_r$ contains a non-constant function;
3. $\mathcal F^{\rm harm}_r$ contains a non-constant function;
4. $\mathcal F^{\rm an}_r$ contains a non-constant function;
5. $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is finite.

Consequently:
- $r_A^{\rm hol}(n)=r_A^{\rm harm}(n)=r_A^{\rm an}(n)=\sec(\pi/n)=2/|1+\zeta_n|$;
- the germ classes $\bar{\mathcal F}^{\mathcal C}_r$ are non-trivial iff $r<\sec(\pi/n)$;
- $r_A(n)^2=2/(1+\cos(2\pi/n)) \in \mathbb Q(\zeta_n)^+$.

The implications (2)⇒(3)⇒(4) are immediate: take the real part of a holomorphic function, and
note that harmonic functions are real-analytic. The remaining implications are proved as follows:
- (1)⇒(5) and (1)⇒(2): §5.
- (4)⇒(1), in contrapositive form: §6.
- (5)⇒(1): §8.

The germ statement then follows from Lemma 3.3(2).

**Exact values.**

| $n$ | $r_A(n)$ | $r_A(n)^2$ | numerical |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | $\sqrt5-1=2/\varphi$ | $6-2\sqrt5$ | 1.236068 |
| 7 | $\sec(\pi/7)$ | root of $y^3-24y^2+80y-64$ | 1.109916 |
| 8 | $\sqrt{4-2\sqrt2}$ | $4-2\sqrt2$ | 1.082392 |
| 10 | $\sec(\pi/10)$ | $2-\tfrac{2}{5}\sqrt5$ | 1.051462 |
| 12 | $\sqrt6-\sqrt2$ | $8-4\sqrt3$ | 1.035276 |

The cubic for $n=7$ comes from substituting $c = 2/y - 1$ into $8c^3+4c^2-4c-1=0$, the minimal
polynomial of $c = \cos(2\pi/7)$.

## 5. Below the necklace: $P_r$ is full and the quotient is an open Riemann surface

Throughout this section $1<r\le\sec(\pi/n)$ and $n\ge5$.

**Lemma 5.1 (short words)** [proved].
1. $A^kL\cap D_2=\emptyset$ and $B^kL\cap D_1=\emptyset$ for $k\not\equiv0$.
2. A word with non-empty domain, after reduction, has at most two blocks. Here reduction means
   cancelling adjacent inverse letters, merging adjacent powers of the same generator, and deleting
   blocks $A^{mn}$ or $B^{mn}$. Each of these operations leaves the motion unchanged and can only
   enlarge the domain. Since $\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma)$ is the union over all words, it equals the union
   over reduced words. So the motions with non-empty $\mathrm{Dom}_P$ are:

| motion | $\mathrm{Dom}_P$ |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{id}$ | $\Omega_r$ |
| $A^k$ | $D_1$ |
| $B^j$ | $D_2$ |
| $B^jA^k$ | $A^{-k}L$ |
| $A^kB^j$ | $B^{-j}L$ |

Here $j,k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}$.

*Proof.*
1. $A^kL\cap D_2=D_1\cap D_2\cap A^kD_2$ is the intersection of three open disks with centres
   $-1,1,A^k(1)$. It is empty by Lemmas 1.1 and 1.2, since $r\le\sec(\pi/n)$. The same argument
   applies to $B$.
2. Consider a word with three blocks, say $A^iB^jA^k$. A point $z$ of its domain satisfies
   $A^kz\in D_1\cap D_2=L$, so $B^jA^kz\in B^jL$. But this point must lie in $D_1$, contradicting (1).
   The symmetric case $B^iA^jB^k$ is the same.
3. The domains in the table follow from the definitions; for example
   $\mathrm{Dom}(B^jA^k)=D_1\cap A^{-k}D_2=A^{-k}L$. ∎

**Coincidences.** $B^jA^k=A^{k'}B^{j'}$ in $\Gamma$ happens iff $n$ is even, $k=n/2-j$, $k'=j+n/2$ and
$j'=-j$, with $j\ne n/2$.

*Derivation.* Comparing $z\mapsto\zeta^{-j-k}z+\zeta^{-j-k}-2\zeta^{-j}+1$ with
$z\mapsto\zeta^{-j'-k'}z-\zeta^{-j'-k'}+2\zeta^{-k'}-1$ forces
$1+\zeta^{-m}=\zeta^{-j}+\zeta^{-k'}$ with $m = j + k$. The only solutions with $j,k,k' \not\equiv 0$
are $\zeta^{-m}=-1$ and $\zeta^{-k'}=-\zeta^{-j}$. No other pairs in the table coincide: an equation
$B^i=A^l$ forces both sides to be the identity.

So the number of motions is $1+2(n-1)+2(n-1)^2=2n^2-2n+1$ for odd $n$, and $2n^2-3n+3$ for even
$n$ **[proved]**. The hull computation of §10 reproduces both counts.

The **translation germs** in $P_r$ are the $2(n-1)$ motions
$B^{-k}A^k: z\mapsto z+2(1-\zeta^k)$ on $A^{-k}L$ and $A^{k}B^{-k}: z\mapsto z-2(1-\zeta^{-k})$ on
$B^{k}L$. Their vectors span the dense group $T$, but their domains are pairwise disjoint.

**Lemma 5.2 ($P_r$ is full)** [proved]. Each $\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma)$ is a union of components of
$\Omega_r\cap\gamma^{-1}\Omega_r$. Hence $H_r=P_r$ and $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is finite.

*Proof.*
1. **$\gamma=A^k$.** $\Omega_r\cap A^{-k}\Omega_r=D_1\cup(D_2\cap A^{-k}D_2)$. The two pieces are
   disjoint, because $D_1\cap D_2\cap A^{-k}D_2$ is empty (centres $-1,1,A^{-k}(1)$, Lemma 1.2). So
   $D_1$ is a component. The case $B^j$ is symmetric.
2. **$\gamma=B^jA^k$.** The domain is the atom $(1,2)$, namely $D_1\cap A^{-k}D_2=A^{-k}L$. It meets
   none of the other three atoms:
   - **with $(1,1)$:** applying $A^k$ to the intersection gives $L\cap B^{-j}D_1$, the intersection of
     disks centred at $-1,1,B^{-j}(-1)$;
   - **with $(2,2)$:** the intersection is $D_1\cap D_2\cap A^{-k}D_2$, disks centred at
     $-1,1,A^{-k}(1)$;
   - **with $(2,1)$:** the intersection is contained in the previous one.

   All of these are empty by Lemma 1.2. So $A^{-k}L$ is a component on its own.
3. **The rest.** The case $A^kB^j$ is symmetric. A coincident motion has as domain the union of two
   such components, and $\mathrm{id}$ has domain $\Omega_r$. ∎

**Lemma 5.3 (Hausdorff quotient)** [proved]. Let $\mathcal P$ be a full collection with finitely many
motions. Then the orbit space $Q=\Omega_r/\mathcal P$ is Hausdorff.

*Proof.*
1. The relation is open: the saturation of an open $V$ is $\bigcup_\gamma\gamma(V\cap U_\gamma)$. So
   it suffices to show that the graph $R=\{(z,\gamma z):z\in U_\gamma\}$ is closed in
   $\Omega_r\times\Omega_r$.
2. Let $(z_k,\gamma_kz_k)\to(p,q)$ with $p,q \in \Omega_r$. Since there are finitely many motions,
   pass to a subsequence with $\gamma_k=\gamma$ constant. Then $q=\gamma p$, so
   $p\in\Omega_r\cap\gamma^{-1}\Omega_r$.
3. Let $C$ be the component of $p$ in $\Omega_r\cap\gamma^{-1}\Omega_r$. It is an open neighbourhood
   of $p$, so it contains $z_k\in U_\gamma$ for large $k$.
4. By fullness $C\subset U_\gamma$, so $p\in U_\gamma$ and $(p,q)\in R$. ∎

**Lemma 5.4 (Riemann surface structure)** [proved]. Under the hypotheses of Lemma 5.3, $Q$ is a
connected Riemann surface and $\pi:\Omega_r\to Q$ is holomorphic in the charts below.

*Proof.*
1. **Isotropy.** For $p\in\Omega_r$, let $G_p=\{\gamma: p\in U_\gamma,\ \gamma p=p\}$. It is a finite
   group of rotations about $p$, hence cyclic of some order $m$.
2. **A good disk.** Choose $\varepsilon>0$ such that:
   - $D(p,\varepsilon)\subset U_\gamma$ for every $\gamma\in G_p$;
   - no $\gamma\notin G_p$ moves a point of $D(p,\varepsilon)\cap U_\gamma$ into $D(p,\varepsilon)$.

   The second condition can be met. Otherwise there would be $z_k\to p$ with $\gamma z_k\to p$; the
   argument of Lemma 5.3 then gives $p\in U_\gamma$ and $\gamma p=p$, so $\gamma\in G_p$.
3. **Charts.** $D(p,\varepsilon)/G_p\to Q$ is an open embedding. The function $w=(z-p)^m$ is a chart
   on it. For $m>1$, shrink $\varepsilon$ so that $D(p,\varepsilon)$ contains no other point with
   non-trivial isotropy; then $[p]$ lies in no other chart of this kind.
   - On an overlap of two charts not containing a cone point, the transition is
     $w\mapsto\big(\gamma(p+w^{1/m})-p'\big)^{m'}$ for a single-valued branch of $w^{1/m}$, with
     $\gamma\in\mathcal P$. Either $m=1$, or the overlap lies in the punctured disk, where the branch
     is holomorphic.
   - So the transitions are holomorphic.
4. **Connectedness and second countability.** $Q$ is connected because $\Omega_r$ is. It is second
   countable because $\pi$ is open, so the images of a countable base of $\Omega_r$ form a base of
   $Q$. ∎

**Lemma 5.5 (non-compactness)** [proved]. For $1<r\le\sec(\pi/n)$ the surface $Q=\Omega_r/P_r$ is not
compact.

*Proof.*
1. **Test points.** For $s<r$ let $z_s=-1+se^{i\pi/n}$. Its images $A^kz_s$ are the points
   $-1+se^{i\theta}$ with $\theta=\pi/n+2\pi k/n$.
2. **They avoid $\bar D_2$.** For each such $\theta$,
   $|{-1}+se^{i\theta}-1|^2 \ge s^2+4-4s\cos(\pi/n)$.
   - The right side is decreasing in $s$ for $s<2\cos(\pi/n)$, and $r<2\cos(\pi/n)$ for $n\ge5$.
   - At $s=r$ it is at least $r^2$, because $r\cos(\pi/n)\le1$.

   So for $s<r$ every $A^kz_s$ lies outside $\bar D_2$, and the class of $z_s$ is $\{A^kz_s\}$.
3. **They approach the boundary.** The radial boundary point $-1+re^{i\theta}$ lies in
   $\partial\Omega_r$ by the same inequality. So every member of the class is within $r-s$ of
   $\partial\Omega_r$.
4. **Contradiction.** If $Q$ were compact, then $Q=\pi(K)$ for a compact $K\subset\Omega_r$, since
   $\pi$ is open and $\Omega_r$ is exhausted by compact sets. Every class would then meet $K$. This
   is false for $s$ close to $r$. ∎

**Proof of (1)⇒(2) and (1)⇒(5).**
1. $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is finite by Lemma 5.2.
2. $Q=\Omega_r/P_r$ is a non-compact connected Riemann surface by Lemmas 5.3–5.5.
3. **Behnke–Stein theorem** (H. Behnke and K. Stein, *Math. Ann.* 120 (1949)). Every connected
   non-compact Riemann surface is a Stein manifold; in particular it carries a non-constant
   holomorphic function. This gives a non-constant holomorphic $F$ on $Q$.

   *Alternative, by uniformisation.* Let $C_i=\pi(D_i)$; these are open subsets of $Q$.
   - $C_1\cong D_1/C_n$, because two points of $D_1$ are equivalent only through a power of $A$: by
     Lemma 5.1, $B^jA^k$ maps $D_1$-points into $B^jL$, which is disjoint from $D_1$. So $C_1$ is
     homeomorphic to a disk, and likewise $C_2$.
   - $C_1\cap C_2=\pi(L)\cong L$, since $L\cap A^kL=\emptyset$ for $k\ne0$. This is connected.
   - By van Kampen, $Q$ is simply connected. It is non-compact by Lemma 5.5.
   - So by uniformisation $Q$ is biholomorphic to the unit disk or to $\mathbb C$, and the
     uniformising coordinate is a non-constant holomorphic $F$.
4. $f=F\circ\pi$ is holomorphic on $\Omega_r$. It is invariant because $\pi\circ A=\pi$ on $D_1$ and
   $\pi\circ B=\pi$ on $D_2$. It is non-constant because $\pi$ is onto. ∎

*Remark (structure of $Q$).*
- The only points with non-trivial isotropy are $-1$ and $1$, each of order $n$. A fixed point of
  $B^jA^k$ would have to lie in $A^{-k}L\subset D_1$ and also in $B^jL$, which is disjoint from $D_1$.
- So $Q$ is the union of two flat cones $D_1/C_n$ and $D_2/C_n$, glued along a copy of the lens. It
  is simply connected and non-compact (step 3 above), hence homeomorphic to an open disk.

## 6. Above the necklace: every invariant function is constant

Throughout this section $r>\sec(\pi/n)$, $n=5$ or $n\ge7$, and $f\in\mathcal F^{\rm an}_r$.

**Step 1 (a translation identity on a whole atom).**
1. For $z\in U_{21}:=D_2\cap(D_1-t)$ we have $Bz\in D_1$, since $z+t=A^{-1}Bz$ and $A^{-1}$
   preserves $D_1$. Hence
   $$f(z+t)=f(A^{-1}Bz)=f(Bz)=f(z).$$
   $U_{21}\ne\emptyset$ for $r>1$, because its centres $1$ and $1-2\zeta$ are at distance 2.
2. The convex atom $U_{11}:=D_1\cap(D_1-t)$ meets $U_{21}$. Their intersection is the intersection of
   the disks centred at $-1,1,1-2\zeta$, which is non-empty by Lemma 1.2 since $r>\sec(\pi/n)$.
3. So $U_{11}\cup U_{21}$ is a connected open subset of $\Omega_r\cap(\Omega_r-t)$. The real-analytic
   function $f(\cdot+t)-f$ vanishes on $U_{21}$, hence on $U_{11}$.

This is the only step that uses $r>\sec(\pi/n)$, and it is exactly where $P_r$ fails to be full:
$P_r$ contains $z \mapsto z+t$ on $U_{21}$ but not on the rest of its component.

**Step 2 (the translation set).** Let $S$ be the set of $s\in\mathbb C$ such that $f(z+s)=f(z)$ for
all $z\in D_1\cap(D_1-s)$. Then:
1. $t\in S$, by Step 1.
2. $-S=S$: substitute $w=z+s$.
3. $\zeta^{j}S=S$.
   - Write $A^{-k}(w)=\zeta^k(w+1)-1$. Then $A^{-k}(z+\zeta^{-k}s)=A^{-k}z+s$, so $A^{-k}$ maps
     $D_1\cap(D_1-\zeta^{-k}s)$ onto $D_1\cap(D_1-s)$.
   - Hence, for $z$ in the first set, $f(z+\zeta^{-k}s)=f(A^{-k}z+s)=f(A^{-k}z)=f(z)$.
4. **Sums.** Suppose $s_1,s_2\in S$ and $\operatorname{mec}(0,s_1,s_1+s_2)<r$. Then $s_1+s_2\in S$.
   - The set $W=D_1\cap(D_1-s_1)\cap(D_1-s_1-s_2)$ is the intersection of the disks centred at
     $-1,-1-s_1,-1-s_1-s_2$. It is non-empty by Lemma 1.1.
   - On $W$: $f(z+s_1+s_2)=f(z+s_1)=f(z)$.
   - $W$ is open and lies in the convex set $D_1\cap(D_1-s_1-s_2)$, so the identity theorem applies.

**Step 3 (a contracting multiplier).**
- For $n\ge7$, take $\mu=1-\zeta$, with $|\mu|=2\sin(\pi/n)<1$.
- For $n=5$, take $\mu=1+\zeta^2$, with $|\mu|=2\cos(2\pi/5)=1/\varphi<1$.

**Claim:** if $s\in S$ and $|s|\le|t|$, then $s\mu\in S$.
1. **$n\ge7$.** By Step 2, $-\zeta s\in S$. The triangle $(0,\,s,\,s-\zeta s)$ is isosceles, with
   apex $s$, legs $|s|$ and apex angle $2\pi/n$. It is acute, so
   $$\operatorname{mec}=\frac{|s|}{2\cos(\pi/n)}\le\frac{4\sin(\pi/n)}{2\cos(\pi/n)}=2\tan\frac{\pi}{n}<\sec\frac{\pi}{n}<r .$$
   The middle inequality is equivalent to $2\sin(\pi/n)<1$, i.e. $n>6$.
2. **$n=5$.** By Step 2, $\zeta^2s\in S$. The triangle $(0,\,s,\,s+\zeta^2 s)$ is isosceles, with
   apex $s$, legs $|s|$ and apex angle $\pi/5$ (the angle between $-1$ and $\zeta^2$). It is acute, so
   $$\operatorname{mec}=\frac{|s|}{2\cos(\pi/10)}\le\frac{4\sin(\pi/5)}{2\cos(\pi/10)}=4\sin\frac{\pi}{10}=\sqrt5-1=\sec\frac{\pi}{5}<r .$$
   The bound equals $r_A(5)$ exactly, a small coincidence of the pentagon.

By induction $s_k:=t\mu^k\in S$ for all $k\ge0$. The $s_k$ are non-zero and $s_k\to0$.

**Step 4 (constancy).**

**Lemma 6.1** [proved]. Let $f$ be real-analytic on the connected set $\Omega_r$ with $f\circ A=f$ on
$D_1$. Suppose there are a ball $\mathcal B\subset\Omega_r$ and non-zero $s_k\to0$ with
$f(z+s_k)=f(z)$ for all $z\in\mathcal B$. Then $f$ is constant.

*Proof.*
1. Pass to a subsequence with $s_k/|s_k|\to u$. Then
   $0=(f(z+s_k)-f(z))/|s_k|\to\partial_uf(z)$ on $\mathcal B$.
2. $\partial_uf$ is real-analytic on $\Omega_r$, so $\partial_u f\equiv0$ on $\Omega_r$.
3. Differentiating $f\circ A=f$ gives $\partial_{\zeta^{-1}u}f(Az)=\partial_uf(z)=0$ for $z\in D_1$. So
   $\partial_{\zeta^{-1}u}f=0$ on $A(D_1)=D_1$, hence on $\Omega_r$.
4. The directions $u$ and $\zeta^{-1}u$ are $\mathbb R$-independent, so $df\equiv0$. ∎

For $|s_k|\le r/2$, the set $D_1\cap(D_1-s_k)$ contains $\mathcal B=D(-1,r/2)$. Lemma 6.1 then shows
that $f$ is constant. This proves (4)⇒(1) in contrapositive form. ∎

## 8. The hull above the necklace, and the gap

**Theorem 8.1** [proved]. Let $n=5$ or $n\ge7$.
1. For $1<r\le\sec(\pi/n)$, $P_r$ is full, $H_r=P_r$, and $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)=\Gamma(r)$ is finite,
   with the counts of §5. Here $\Gamma(r)$ is the set of motions realized by $GG_n(r)$ (closed
   disks) on pieces with non-empty interior.
2. For $r>\sec(\pi/n)$ the following hold.
   - $H_r$ contains the translation by $s$ on $D_1\cap(D_1-s)$ for every $s$ in a set
     $S_H \subset \mathbb C$.
   - $S_H \cap D(0,\varepsilon)$ is dense in $D(0,\varepsilon)$ for some $\varepsilon>0$.
   - Hence $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is infinite, and the $H_r$-orbit of every point of $D(-1,r/2)$ is
     dense in a disk about that point.

*Proof.*
1. **Part 1.** $H_r=P_r$ and finiteness are Lemmas 5.1 and 5.2. Since $H_r=P_r$, the claim
   $\Gamma(r)=\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ follows once we show that
   $\Gamma(r)=\{\gamma:\mathrm{Dom}_P(\gamma)\ne\emptyset\}$. That identity holds at every $r>1$.
   - **Inclusion $\supseteq$.** A word $w$ with $\mathrm{Dom}(w)\ne\emptyset$ acts, as an element of
     $GG_n(r)$, by $\rho(w)$ on the open set $\mathrm{Dom}(w)$. Each step there applies a generator to
     a point of the open disk.
   - **Inclusion $\subseteq$.** Let an element $w=s_m\cdots s_1$ of $GG_n(r)$ act by $\gamma$ on an
     open set $V$. For each $i$, the set of $z\in V$ such that $s_{i-1}\cdots s_1z$ lies on the
     boundary circle relevant to $s_i$ is closed and nowhere dense: it is contained in finitely many
     circles, since $s_{i-1}\cdots s_1$ is a piecewise motion with finitely many pieces.
   - Pick $z$ off all these sets, and a small disk $V'\ni z$ that also avoids them. Along the path of
     every point of $V'$, each letter either acts on a point of the open disk or fixes a point
     outside the closed disk, and which case occurs is the same for all points of $V'$.
   - Deleting the letters that act as the identity gives a word $w'$ with $V'\subset\mathrm{Dom}(w')$
     and $\rho(w')=\gamma$.
2. **Part 2.** Steps 1–3 of §6 use only hull operations: composition,
   inverse and extension to a component. They never use $f$. So $S_H\ni\zeta^jt\mu^k$ for all $j$ and
   all $k\ge0$, and $S_H$ satisfies the rules of Step 2.
3. Fix $K$ with $|t\mu^K|<\varepsilon$, where $8\varepsilon<r$.
4. **Generated group.** The group generated by $\{\zeta^jt\mu^K\}_j$ is $t\mu^K\mathbb Z[\zeta]$. This
   is dense in $\mathbb C$: its closure is a closed subgroup of $\mathbb R^2$ invariant under rotation
   by $2\pi/n$ with $n\notin\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, and it is not discrete because its rank $\varphi(n)$
   exceeds 2.
5. **Sums are realized.** Let $x$ lie in this group with $|x|<\varepsilon$, and write it as a sum of
   generators $v_1,\dots,v_N$, each of modulus less than $\varepsilon$.
   - By the Steinitz lemma, the vectors $v_1,\dots,v_N,-x$ can be ordered so that every partial sum
     has modulus at most $2\varepsilon$. (The Steinitz constant of $\mathbb R^d$ is at most $d$; in
     the Euclidean plane it is $\sqrt5/2$, by Banaszczyk.)
   - Starting the cyclic order just after $-x$ gives an ordering of the $v_i$ whose partial sums
     have modulus at most $4\varepsilon$.
   - Each step of Step 2 rule 4 is then applied to a triangle whose vertices are $0$ and two
     consecutive partial sums, all within $4\varepsilon$ of $0$. So its mec is at most
     $4\varepsilon<r$. Hence $x\in S_H$.
6. So $S_H\cap D(0,\varepsilon)$ is dense in $D(0,\varepsilon)$. For $z\in D(-1,r/2)$, the
   $H_r$-orbit contains $z+(S_H\cap D(0,\varepsilon))$. ∎

**Proof of (5)⇒(1) in Theorem 4.1.** This is the contrapositive of part 2. ∎

**Corollary 8.2 (fullness fails at $r_A$)** [proved]. $P_r$ is full for $r\le\sec(\pi/n)$. For every
$r>\sec(\pi/n)$ at which the $GG_n(r)$-orbits of the points of $D(-1,r/2)$ are finite, $P_r$ is not
full.

*Proof.* $P_r$-orbits are contained in $GG_n(r)$-orbits: a word whose intermediate points lie in the
open disks acts as the corresponding element of $GG_n(r)$. If $P_r$ were full, it would equal $H_r$
and have the dense orbits of Theorem 8.1. ∎

**Corollary 8.3 (the gap)** [proved]. Let $r_c(n)$ be the true critical radius, the least $r$ at which
$GG_n(r)$ is infinite. Then for every $r\in(r_A(n),r_c(n))$:
- $GG_n(r)$ is finite, the realized set $\Gamma(r)$ is finite, and every $P_r$-orbit is finite;
- $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is infinite, $H_r$ has orbits dense in disks, and every invariant
  real-analytic function is constant.

*Proof.*
1. $GG_n(r)$ is finite for every $r<r_c$, by group monotonicity (`boundary-contact.md`,
   Theorem 3.4), which also makes $r_c$ well defined.
2. $\Gamma(r)$ is finite because each element of a finite $GG_n(r)$ has finitely many pieces.
3. The remaining statements are Theorems 8.1 and 4.1. ∎

That is the precise form of "the group is finite although the germs it contains generate, under
analytic continuation, a dense group".

**Whether the interval is non-empty.** This is the statement $r_A<r_c$.
- For $n=5$ it is **proved modulo a certified computation**.
  - Theorem B of `critical-radius-n5.md` (refereed) proves $GG_5(r)$ finite for
    $r\le r_\ast\approx2.12557$, from an exact cut-set computation combined with group monotonicity.
    This is a radius above $r_A(5)=1.236$. Hence $r_c(5)>r_A(5)$.
  - Theorem A of that note shows $GG_5(r)$ infinite for $r\ge\sqrt{3+\varphi}$. Finiteness just
    below $\sqrt{3+\varphi}$ is open there.
- For $n=8,10,12$ it is likewise **proved modulo certified computations**: the certificates recorded
  in [`../certificates.md`](../certificates.md) give finiteness at radii $1.70$, $1.53$, $1.37$, above
  $r_A=1.082$, $1.051$, $1.035$.
- For the other $n$ it rests on the paper's Appendix A table [computed]. The table records the radii
  at which very large or infinite orbits were found. Whether those are true critical radii is the
  large-$n$ question of `../overview.md` §5.
- Proposition 9.1 gives $r_c\ge r_A$ for every $n\ge5$ [proved].

## 9. The group $GG_n(r)$ below the necklace

**Proposition 9.1** [proved]. Let $n\ge5$ and $1\le r<\sec(\pi/n)$ (closed disks).
1. Every orbit of $GG_n(r)$ is of one of four kinds:
   - a fixed point (including the centres $\pm1$);
   - $\{A^kz\}$, with $n$ points in $\bar D_1\setminus\bar D_2$;
   - $\{B^jz\}$, with $n$ points in $\bar D_2\setminus\bar D_1$;
   - a **lens orbit** $X_z=\{z\}\cup\{A^kz\}_{k\ne0}\cup\{B^jz\}_{j\ne0}$ with $2n-1$ points, for
     $z\in\bar L$.
2. For every such $r$, $GG_n(r)$ is isomorphic to the image of $C_n * C_n$ in $C_n\times C_n\times\mathrm{Sym}(X)$, with
   $|X| = 2n-1$, under
   $$a\mapsto(1,0,\sigma_a), \qquad b\mapsto(0,1,\sigma_b),$$
   where $\sigma_a,\sigma_b$ are $n$-cycles sharing exactly one point.
3. $|GG_n(r)|=n^2(2n-1)!/2$. The group is $C_n\times C_n\times A_{2n-1}$ for odd $n$, and the index-2
   subgroup $\{(i,j,\pi):\operatorname{sgn}\pi=(-1)^{i+j}\}$ of $C_n\times C_n\times S_{2n-1}$ for
   even $n$.
4. For $r>\sec(\pi/n)$, generic points of $L\cap A(L) \neq \emptyset$ have orbits with at least
   $3n-2$ points.

*Proof.*
1. **Orbits.** The closed version of Lemma 5.1 holds for $r<\sec(\pi/n)$: closed disks with
   $\operatorname{mec}>r$ have empty intersection. So $A^k\bar L\cap\bar D_2=\emptyset$ and
   $B^j\bar L\cap\bar D_1=\emptyset$ for $k,j\ne0$. Part 1 follows, and the $2n-1$ points of a lens
   orbit are distinct.
2. **Faithful image.** Label every lens orbit by the same set $X$. Then $a$ and $b$ act on all lens
   orbits by the same permutations $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_b$. On the other orbits they act through
   the exponent sums modulo $n$. An element is determined by these actions, which gives part 2.
3. **The permutation image.** Let $G_X=\langle\sigma_a,\sigma_b\rangle$.
   - $G_X$ is transitive.
   - $G_X$ is primitive. Let $\Delta$ be a block containing the common point $x_0$. A block through a
     point fixed by $\sigma_a$ is $\sigma_a$-invariant, so it contains all of $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma_a$
     or none of it; the same holds for $\sigma_b$. Suppose $\Delta$ contains a point $y\ne x_0$.
     - Every point other than $x_0$ is fixed by $\sigma_a$ or by $\sigma_b$; say $y$ is fixed by
       $\sigma_a$.
     - Then $\Delta$ is $\sigma_a$-invariant and contains $x_0\in\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma_a$, so it
       contains $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma_a$.
     - So it contains a point of $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma_a\setminus\{x_0\}$, which is fixed by
       $\sigma_b$. Then $\Delta$ is $\sigma_b$-invariant and contains $\mathrm{supp}\,\sigma_b$.
     - Hence $\Delta=X$. The case of $y$ fixed by $\sigma_b$ is symmetric.
   - The commutator of two cycles meeting in exactly one point is a 3-cycle.
   - By Jordan's theorem (a primitive group containing a 3-cycle contains the alternating group;
     Wielandt, *Finite Permutation Groups*, Thm. 13.3, or Dixon–Mortimer, *Permutation Groups*,
     Thm. 3.3A; neither theorem number has been confirmed), $G_X\supseteq A_X$.
     So $G_X=A_X$ for odd $n$ (both $n$-cycles are even) and $G_X=S_X$ for even $n$.
4. **The whole group.** The image $G$ projects onto $C_n^2$ and onto $G_X$. The kernel of the
   projection to $C_n^2$ maps onto a normal subgroup of $G_X$ with abelian quotient, which contains
   $A_X$ (simple, since $|X|\ge9$). Goursat's lemma then gives part 3: the generators satisfy
   $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma_a=\operatorname{sgn}\sigma_b=(-1)^{n-1}$.
5. **Above the threshold.** For part 4, take $z\in L\cap A(L)$ (non-empty by Lemma 1.2). Its orbit
   contains:
   - the $n$ points $A^kz$;
   - the $n-1$ points $B^jz$ with $j\ne0$;
   - the $n-1$ points $B^jA^{-1}z$ with $j\ne0$.

   These $3n-2$ points are distinct unless $z$ is fixed by one of finitely many non-trivial elements
   of $\Gamma$. A non-trivial motion fixes at most one point. ∎

The paper's Figure 3b reports $C_5\times C_5\times A_9$ for a small overlap, which is part 3 for
$n=5$. Proposition 9.1 adds two things:
- the isomorphism type is the same on all of $[1,\sec(\pi/n))$;
- the orbit structure first changes after $r=1$ exactly at $r_A$.

Part 4 does not by itself show that the abstract group changes, because orbits of $3n-2$ points are
compatible with a group of order $n^2(2n-1)!/2$. Whether $GG_n(r)\not\cong GG_n(1)$ for $r$ just
above $r_A$ is **open**.
- The referee's floating-point computations for $n=5,7,8$ just above $r_A$ find induced permutation
  groups of order far above $n^2(2n-1)!/2$. This is evidence only.
- A proof could exhibit an orbit whose size does not divide that order.

## 10. Thresholds, numbers and computations

### 10.1 Which threshold is which

| | threshold | meaning | label |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | $r=1$ | the disks meet (open disks overlap for $r>1$) | — |
| (ii) | $r=1$ | $GG_n(r)\ne C_n\times C_n$, since $[a,b]0=A(0)\ne0$ at $r=1$; for $1\le r<r_A$ it is the group of Proposition 9.1 | [proved] |
| (iii) | $r>1$ | $P_r$ contains a translation germ ($a^{-1}b$ on $B^{-1}L$) | [proved] |
| (iv) | $r>r_A=\sec(\pi/n)$ | the necklace closes. Equivalently: two translation germs with non-discrete span act on a common open set; $\Gamma^{\rm an}(r)$ is infinite; invariant analytic functions are constant. Also: the orbit structure of $GG_n(r)$ first changes after $r=1$, and $P_r$ stops being full wherever $GG_n(r)$ is finite. Whether the abstract group changes here is open | [proved] |
| (v) | $r=g(n)=\lvert 2-\zeta\rvert$ | the orbits of 0 about the two centres touch ($B(0)\in\partial D_1$). Equivalently, $D_1$ meets $D_2-t$, i.e. $\operatorname{mec}(-1,\,B^{-1}A(1))=g$ | [proved] as geometry |
| (vi) | $r_c(n)$ | $GG_n(r)$ becomes infinite | paper |

- **(iii) and (iv) differ.** Below $r_A$ the $2(n-1)$ translation germs sit on the pairwise disjoint
  sets $A^{-k}L$ and $B^kL$. Conjugating $z\mapsto z+t$ by $A^k$ would need the lens image
  $B^{-1}L$ to meet $D_1$, which is the same triple condition as the necklace.
- **The question in the brief.** Conjugation by the rotations does give the vectors $\zeta^kt$, but
  only once the identity theorem has carried the germ across the triple overlap.
- **Ordering.** For $n\ge5$, $\sec(\pi/n) < g(n)$, because $\tan^2(\pi/n) < 8\sin^2(\pi/n)$, i.e.
  $\cos^2(\pi/n) > 1/8$. So (iv) comes strictly before (v).
- **A common form.** All three of $r_A$, $g$ and the closed forms of $r_c$ have squares in
  $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)^+$. Both $r_A$ and $g$ are minimal-enclosing radii of two or three points of
  the orbit $\Gamma\{\pm1\}$.

### 10.3 Computations

These are exploratory floating-point scripts, not part of this repository. Motions were stored
exactly in $\mathbb Z[\zeta_n]$ modulo $\Phi_n$; disk-intersection tests were done in double
precision. None of the proofs depends on them.

1. **Hull closure.** The rules of Definition 3.4 (extension, inverse, composition, on atoms) were run
   for $n=5,7,8,10,12$.
   - At $r=(1+r_A)/2$ and at $r=r_A-10^{-4}$, the closure terminates with exactly the counts of §5:
     41, 85, 107, 173 and 255 motions.
   - At $r=r_A+10^{-4}$ and $r=r_A+10^{-2}$, it grows past the cap of 500 motions. It contains
     translations of modulus 0.28, 0.96, 0.26, 0.29 and 0.28 (below the cap), compared with the
     smallest translation $|t| = 4\sin(\pi/n)$ available below $r_A$.
2. **Group order.** At $r=(1+r_A)/2$ the permutation group generated on sample orbits (one lens
   orbit, one $D_1$-only orbit and one $D_2$-only orbit) has order exactly $n^2(2n-1)!/2$ for all five
   $n$.
3. **Orbit sizes near $r_A$.** At $r_A\pm10^{-3}$, random lens points have orbits of $2n-1$ points.
   Just above $r_A$, points of $L\cap A(L)$ have orbits of 13–40 points ($n=5$) up to 45–132
   ($n=12$). These are larger than $2n-1$ but finite, and show no sign of the group becoming
   infinite.

**Notes on the proofs.**
1. **Boundary conventions.** Section 5 uses *open* disks; with open disks, an intersection at the
   circumcentre is empty at $r=\sec(\pi/n)$, which is why $r=r_A$ is included there.
   Proposition 9.1 uses *closed* disks and needs $r<\sec(\pi/n)$ strictly: at $r = r_A$ the lens
   corners $\pm i\tan(\pi/n)$ are exchanged by $A$, and their orbits are of a new kind.
2. **Fullness and the Hausdorff property.** Lemma 5.3 needs finitely many motions and fullness. Both
   are checked for $r\le r_A$ by direct enumeration (Lemmas 5.1, 5.2). The noncompactness argument
   (Lemma 5.5) is specific to $r\le r_A$. A general argument via orbifold Gauss–Bonnet would apply to
   any finite full hull; it is not needed.
3. **Three conditions used in §6.** The constancy proof uses $r>r_A$ only once, in Step 1. Step 3
   needs $|s|\le|t|$; this holds because $|\mu|<1$. Lemma 6.1 needs a ball common to infinitely many
   identities, which is $D(-1,r/2)$.
4. **Cited results.** Behnke–Stein (1949), Jordan's 3-cycle theorem and the Steinitz lemma are
   classical, and the statements used are standard. Textbook section and theorem numbers are not
   confirmed. The Behnke–Stein step can be replaced by uniformisation (§5).
5. **Laplace eigenfunctions** ($\Delta f+\lambda f=0$ with $\lambda > 0$, the class $E_r(\lambda)$ of
   [`../analytic-continuation.md`](../analytic-continuation.md) §4.3).
   - $E_r(\lambda)=0$ for $r>r_A$ [proved]: a constant eigenfunction with $\lambda>0$ is zero.
   - $E_r(\lambda)\ne0$ for $r\le r_A$ is a **[conjecture]**. A sketch: Runge or Lax–Malgrange
     approximation on the open flat orbifold $Q$. The step that needs care is regularity at the two
     cone points.
6. **Excluded $n$.** For $n\in\{2,3,4,6\}$, $T$ is a lattice. Let
   $T^\ast=\{\omega:\mathrm{Re}(\bar\omega\tau)\in\mathbb Z\ \text{for all}\ \tau\in T\}$ be its dual
   lattice, which is $\zeta$-stable, and fix $\omega\in T^\ast\setminus\{0\}$. Then
   $$f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\cos\!\big(2\pi\,\mathrm{Re}(\overline{\zeta^k\omega}\,(z+1))\big)$$
   is $T$-periodic and $A$-invariant ($A$ permutes the terms), hence $\Gamma$-invariant.
   - It is a trigonometric polynomial with non-zero frequencies $\pm\zeta^k\omega$ and positive
     coefficients that do not cancel, so it is non-constant.
   - So $r_A^{\rm an}=\infty$ for these $n$ [proved].
   - The holomorphic threshold for them (when, if ever, $Q$ becomes compact) is not treated here.
7. **The interval $(r_A,r_c)$.** Corollary 8.3 holds at every $r$ in it, by group monotonicity.
   - That the interval is non-empty, i.e. $r_A<r_c$ for the true $r_c$, is proved for $n=5$ (via
     `critical-radius-n5.md`, modulo its certified cut-set computation), and for $n=8,10,12$
     modulo the certificates of `../certificates.md`.
   - For the other $n$ it rests on the paper's computed table.
   - Proposition 9.1 gives $r_c\ge r_A$ for all $n\ge5$.
8. **Untested.** Behaviour of fullness for large $r$ (above $r_c$) was not examined.

## 12. Interpretation

1. **The analogy, corrected.** Analytic continuation does not act on the group $\Gamma(r)$ of
   realized motions, which already generates the dense group $\Gamma$ for every $r>0$. It acts on
   domains. The pseudogroup of the two rotations is full up to $r_A=\sec(\pi/n)$: each germ already
   lives on a whole component of where it could live, so the identity theorem has nothing to add,
   and the orbit space is an honest open Riemann surface with many functions.
2. **What happens at $r_A$.** At $r_A$ the rotated lenses first overlap. The identity theorem then
   carries the translation $a^{-1}b$ from the small set $B^{-1}L$ across a triple overlap onto the
   large set $D_1\cap(D_1-t)$. Rotation conjugates and sums then produce a dense set of translations,
   and every invariant analytic function dies.
3. **What the piecewise group does instead.** $GG_n(r)$ refuses that one continuation step: it keeps
   $z \mapsto z+t$ on the piece $B^{-1}L$ and does not extend it. It stays finite until $r_c$. On
   $(r_A,r_c)$, finiteness of $GG_n(r)$ is therefore purely a failure of fullness, i.e. of the
   identity theorem. This confirms the inversion suggested in
   [`../analytic-continuation.md`](../analytic-continuation.md) §4.3, with an explicit threshold.
4. **Size of the gap.** In the natural variable $r-1$, the analytic radius sits a factor 4.9–10.7
   below $r_c$ for the tabulated $n = 5$–12, and about a factor 8 below $g(n)$ for large $n$.
5. **What "critical radius" means for large $n$.**
   - $r_A$ does not decide the open question of whether the large-$n$ table entries are true critical
     radii; the hypothesis $r_A=g$ is false.
   - What it does show is that non-discreteness is available to the analytic completion from
     $r_A\approx1+\pi^2/(2n^2)$ onward. So $r_c$ is not the radius at which the dense group "appears".
     It is a statement about a window: whether some orbit of the truncated pseudogroup escapes in
     internal space (see [`../arithmetic.md`](../arithmetic.md) §0).
   - The three radii $r_A, g, r_c$ all have the form $1+\kappa/n^2+\dots$, with $\kappa_A=\pi^2/2$,
     $\kappa_g=4\pi^2$ and $\kappa_c\ge4\pi^2$ (table). Each $\kappa$ is set by small-angle geometry
     of the lens.
   - Whether $\kappa_c=\kappa_g$ exactly is the large-$n$ table question, restated. The analytic
     method cannot see it, because analytic functions are already constant there.
6. **A sharper version of the analogy.** For a function element, the natural boundary is where
   continuation stops. Here the relevant boundary is the set of triple points of the disk
   configuration: continuation stops at a domain boundary only if no third disk covers the corner.
   $r_A$ is the first radius at which a corner is covered.
