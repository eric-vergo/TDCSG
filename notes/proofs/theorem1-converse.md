# Finiteness of two-disk groups when lcm(n₁, n₂) ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}

Authors: Eric Vergo, Claude

**Result and status.** Let $n_1, n_2 \ge 1$ and $m = \operatorname{lcm}(n_1, n_2)$. If $m \in \{1,2,3,4,6\}$, then the two-disk group $GG_{n_1,n_2}(r_1,r_2)$ is finite for **all** radii $r_1, r_2 > 0$, equal or unequal, overlapping or not, with closed disks (the proof also works verbatim for open disks, or one of each). There is an explicit bound: every orbit has at most $N = m\cdot\#\{t\in\mathbb Z[\omega] : |t|\le 2R\}$ points, $R = 1+\max(r_1,r_2)$, $\omega = e^{-2\pi \mathrm i/m}$, and $|GG|$ is bounded by a function of $N$ alone. This is the direction of Theorem 1 of arXiv 2302.12950 that the paper omits. **Status: proved.** The finiteness step has two proofs: §5 via uniformly bounded orbits, and §6 via an invariant finite partition. Both share Lemmas 2.1, 2.2 and 3.1 and the translation bound of Theorem 5.1. We also prove that for $n_1,n_2\ge 2$ the ambient group $\Gamma$ generated by the two full-plane rotations is discrete exactly when $m\in\{2,3,4,6\}$ (Proposition 2.4). One correction to the paper's statement: the "if" direction of Theorem 1 needs $n_1, n_2 \ge 2$. For $n_1 = 1$, $n_2 = 5$ the group is $\langle b\rangle \cong C_5$ for every radius, although $\operatorname{lcm} = 5 \notin \{2,3,4,6\}$ (Remark 8.2). The proofs have been independently refereed. The report is `referee-report-thread1.md` in this directory, and §10 records its verdicts and what it did not check.

## 1. Setting and conventions

Throughout, $\mathrm i$ is the imaginary unit, $c_1 = -1$, $c_2 = +1$, and $r_1, r_2 > 0$.

**Definition 1.1.** $D_k = \{z\in\mathbb C : |z-c_k|\le r_k\}$ for $k=1,2$ (closed disks), and $U = D_1\cup D_2$.

**Definition 1.2.** $\zeta_k = e^{-2\pi\mathrm i/n_k}$. The *global rotations* are
$$A(z) = c_1 + \zeta_1(z-c_1) = \zeta_1 z + (\zeta_1-1),\qquad B(z) = c_2+\zeta_2(z-c_2) = \zeta_2 z + (1-\zeta_2).$$
The *piecewise rotations* are $a = A$ on $D_1$ and $a = \mathrm{id}$ on $\mathbb C\setminus D_1$, and likewise $b = B$ on $D_2$ and $b=\mathrm{id}$ off $D_2$.

Since $A$ is an isometry fixing $c_1$, $A(D_1) = D_1$, so $a$ is a bijection of $\mathbb C$, with inverse $a^{-1} = A^{-1}$ on $D_1$ and $\mathrm{id}$ off $D_1$. The same holds for $b$.

**Definition 1.3.** $G = GG_{n_1,n_2}(r_1,r_2)$ is the subgroup of the group of bijections $\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb C)$ generated by $a$ and $b$. The ambient group $\Gamma = \langle A, B\rangle$ is a subgroup of the group of orientation-preserving isometries of $\mathbb C$.

The paper composes left to right. We use ordinary composition $(gh)(z) = g(h(z))$. The two conventions give anti-isomorphic groups, isomorphic via $g\mapsto g^{-1}$, so finiteness and orders are unaffected.

**Definition 1.4.** $m = \operatorname{lcm}(n_1,n_2)$, $\omega = e^{-2\pi\mathrm i/m}$, $\mu_m = \{\omega^j\}$, and $L = \mathbb Z[\omega]$, the subring of $\mathbb C$ generated by $\omega$.

## 2. The ambient group Γ

**Lemma 2.1.** Every $\gamma\in\Gamma$ has the form $\gamma(z) = uz+t$ with $u\in\mu_m$ and $t\in L$. Moreover $\gamma(L) = L$.

*Proof.* Since $n_k \mid m$, $\zeta_k = \omega^{m/n_k}\in\mu_m\subset L$. So $A(z) = \zeta_1 z + (\zeta_1 - 1)$ and $B(z) = \zeta_2 z + (1-\zeta_2)$ have the stated form. Let $\mathcal A = \{z\mapsto uz+t : u\in\mu_m,\ t\in L\}$.
- $\mathcal A$ is closed under composition: $(u z + t)\circ(u'z+t') = uu'z + (ut'+t)$, and $uu'\in\mu_m$, $ut'+t\in L$ because $L$ is a ring containing $\mu_m$.
- $\mathcal A$ is closed under inversion: the inverse of $z\mapsto uz+t$ is $z\mapsto u^{-1}z - u^{-1}t$, and $u^{-1}\in\mu_m$.

So $\mathcal A$ is a group containing $A$ and $B$, and $\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal A$. Every element of $\mathcal A$ maps $L$ into $L$, and so does its inverse, hence $\gamma(L)=L$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.2 (discreteness of $L$).** If $m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, then for every $\rho\ge 0$ the set $L\cap\{|t|\le\rho\}$ is finite.

*Proof.* Consider the cases.
- $m\in\{1,2\}$: $\omega = \pm 1$ and $L=\mathbb Z$.
- $m=4$: $\omega = -\mathrm i$ and $L=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\mathrm i$.
- $m=3$: $\omega^2 = -\omega-1$. For $m=6$: $\omega^2 = \omega-1$. In both cases $\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\omega$ contains $1$ and $\omega$ and is closed under multiplication (because $\omega^2\in\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\omega$), so $L = \mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\omega$. Here $\operatorname{Im}\omega = \mp\sqrt3/2\neq 0$.

In every case $L\subseteq \mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\eta$ with $\eta\in\{1,\mathrm i,\omega\}$. If $\eta \notin\mathbb R$ and $t = x+y\eta$ ($x,y\in\mathbb Z$) has $|t|\le\rho$, then $|y|\,|\operatorname{Im}\eta| = |\operatorname{Im}t|\le\rho$, and $|x|\le|t|+|y||\eta|\le\rho+|y|$. So $(x,y)$ lies in a finite box. For $L=\mathbb Z$ the claim is immediate. $\square$

**Lemma 2.3.** $\varphi(m)\le 2$ if and only if $m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, where $\varphi$ is Euler's function.

*Proof.* $\varphi(m)=\prod_{p^e\| m}p^{e-1}(p-1)$.
- If a prime $p\ge5$ divides $m$, then $\varphi(m)\ge p-1\ge4$.
- If $9\mid m$, then $\varphi(m)\ge 6$. If $8\mid m$, then $\varphi(m)\ge4$.
- So $\varphi(m)\le2$ forces $m\mid 12$. Among the divisors of 12, $\varphi(12)=4$, while $\varphi$ of $1,2,3,4,6$ is $1,1,2,2,2$. $\square$

Call $\Gamma$ *discrete* if for every $z\in\mathbb C$ and every bounded $K\subset\mathbb C$, the set $\Gamma z\cap K$ is finite. This orbit-based notion is the one used below. For groups of the form $\Gamma\subseteq\mu_m\ltimes\mathbb C$ with finite linear part, it agrees with the usual notion of a discrete subgroup of $\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb C)$; Proposition 2.4 is stated and proved for the orbit-based notion.

**Proposition 2.4.** Let $n_1,n_2\ge2$. Then $\Gamma$ is discrete if and only if $m\in\{2,3,4,6\}$. If $m\notin\{2,3,4,6\}$, then $\Gamma$ contains translations by non-zero vectors of arbitrarily small length.

*Proof.* Suppose first that $m\in\{2,3,4,6\}$. If $\gamma z\in K\subseteq\{|w|\le\rho\}$ with $\gamma = uz+t$ as in Lemma 2.1, then $|t|\le\rho+|z|$. There are at most $m$ choices of $u$ and finitely many of $t$ (Lemma 2.2). So $\Gamma z\cap K$ is finite.

Now suppose $m\notin\{2,3,4,6\}$. Since $n_k\ge2$ we have $m\ge2$, so $m\notin\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.
1. *A non-zero translation.* For rotations $f(z) = c+\alpha(z-c)$ and $g(z) = c'+\beta(z-c')$, the commutator $fgf^{-1}g^{-1}$ has linear part $1$. Evaluating at $c'$:
   - $g^{-1}(c') = c'$ and $f^{-1}(c') = c+\alpha^{-1}(c'-c)$;
   - applying $g$ gives $c'+\beta(c-c')(1-\alpha^{-1})$;
   - applying $f$ gives $c+(c'-c)\bigl(\alpha-\beta(\alpha-1)\bigr)$.

   So $fgf^{-1}g^{-1}$ is translation by $(c'-c)(\alpha-1)(1-\beta)$. For $f=A$, $g=B$ this is $t_0 = 2(\zeta_1-1)(1-\zeta_2)$, which is non-zero because $\zeta_1,\zeta_2\ne1$.
2. *The translation module.* Let $T=\{t : (z\mapsto z+t)\in\Gamma\}$, a subgroup of $\mathbb C$ containing $t_0$. We have $A\circ(z\mapsto z+t)\circ A^{-1} = (z\mapsto z+\zeta_1t)$, and the same with $B$, $\zeta_2$. So $T$ is closed under multiplication by $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_2$, hence by $\mathbb Z[\zeta_1,\zeta_2]$.
3. *Identifying the ring.* Since $\gcd(m/n_1, m/n_2)=1$, there are integers $x, y$ with $x\,m/n_1 + y\,m/n_2 = 1$. Then $\omega = \zeta_1^x\zeta_2^y$, so $\mathbb Z[\zeta_1,\zeta_2] = L$ and $T\supseteq Lt_0$.
4. *Rank.* The cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_m$ is monic with integer coefficients, has degree $\varphi(m)$, and is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ (Gauss for prime $m$, Dedekind/Landau in general). So $1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{\varphi(m)-1}$ is a $\mathbb Z$-basis of $L$, and $L\cong\mathbb Z^{\varphi(m)}$. Multiplication by $t_0\ne0$ is injective, so $Lt_0\cong\mathbb Z^{\varphi(m)}$, and $\varphi(m)\ge 3$ by Lemma 2.3 (in fact $\varphi(m)\ge4$, since $\varphi(m)$ is even for $m\ge3$).
5. *Non-discreteness.* A discrete subgroup of $\mathbb R^2$ is free abelian of rank at most 2 (standard). So $Lt_0$ is not discrete: it has non-zero elements of arbitrarily small modulus, giving the claimed small translations. In particular $z+T\subseteq\Gamma z$ has infinitely many points in a bounded neighbourhood of $z$, so $\Gamma$ is not discrete. $\square$

Proposition 2.4 is used only for context. The finiteness proof uses only Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2.

## 3. Local structure of group elements

**Lemma 3.1.** Every $g\in G$ satisfies $g(U)=U$ and $g(z)=z$ for all $z\notin U$.

*Proof.* Each of $a^{\pm1},b^{\pm1}$ maps $D_1$ onto $D_1$ or $D_2$ onto $D_2$ and fixes everything else, so each maps $U$ onto $U$ and fixes $\mathbb C\setminus U$ pointwise. Both properties are preserved under composition. $\square$

**Lemma 3.2 (germs come from $\Gamma$).** For every $g\in G$ and $z\in\mathbb C$ there is $\gamma\in\Gamma$ with $g(z)=\gamma(z)$. Consequently $Gz\subseteq\Gamma z\cap U$ for $z\in U$, and $Gz=\{z\}$ for $z\notin U$.

*Proof.* For a generator $s\in\{a^{\pm1},b^{\pm1}\}$ and any point $w$, $s(w)=S(w)$ for some $S\in\{\mathrm{id},A^{\pm1},B^{\pm1}\}\subseteq\Gamma$. Write $g=s_k\cdots s_1$ and $w_0 = z$, $w_j = s_j(w_{j-1})$. Then $w_j = S_j(w_{j-1})$ with $S_j\in\Gamma$, so $g(z) = S_k\cdots S_1(z)$. The last two claims follow from this and Lemma 3.1. $\square$

The element $\gamma$ depends on $z$; this is what makes $g$ only a *piecewise* isometry.

## 4. Uniformly bounded orbits force a finite group

The obstacle noted in the task is real: finiteness of each orbit says nothing about finiteness of the group when there are infinitely many orbits. A **uniform** bound does suffice for a finitely generated group.

**Lemma 4.1.** Let $F$ be a group generated by $k$ elements. For each $j\ge1$, $F$ has at most $(j!)^k$ subgroups of index $j$.

*Proof.* Let $H\le F$ have index $j$. Choose a bijection $F/H\to\{1,\dots,j\}$ with $H\mapsto1$. Left multiplication gives a homomorphism $\rho_H\colon F\to\mathrm{Sym}(j)$ with $H = \{w : \rho_H(w)(1)=1\}$. So $H$ is determined by some homomorphism $F\to\mathrm{Sym}(j)$. A homomorphism is determined by the images of the $k$ generators, so there are at most $(j!)^k$ of them. $\square$

**Proposition 4.2.** Let $G$ be a group of bijections of a set $X$, generated by $k$ elements. Suppose every $G$-orbit has at most $N$ elements. Then $G$ is finite, and $|G|\le\prod_{H\in\mathcal H_N}[F:H]$, where $F$ is the free group of rank $k$ and $\mathcal H_N$ is the (finite) set of subgroups of $F$ of index at most $N$.

*Proof.*
1. Let $\pi\colon F\to G$ be the surjection sending the free generators to the given generators. For $x\in X$ put $H_x = \pi^{-1}(\mathrm{Stab}_G(x))$. Then $[F:H_x] = |Gx|\le N$, so $H_x\in\mathcal H_N$.
2. Since $G$ consists of bijections of $X$, $\pi(w)$ is the identity exactly when it fixes every $x$. So $\ker\pi = \bigcap_{x\in X}H_x$.
3. This is an intersection of members of the finite set $\mathcal H_N$ (Lemma 4.1).
4. For subgroups $H,H'$, the map $F/(H\cap H')\to F/H\times F/H'$ is injective, so $[F:H\cap H']\le[F:H][F:H']$. By induction $[F:\ker\pi]\le\prod_{H\in\mathcal H_N}[F:H]$.
5. So $G\cong F/\ker\pi$ is finite with the stated bound. $\square$

The bound is far from sharp. It matters only that it depends on $N$ and $k$ alone.

## 5. Main theorem

**Theorem 5.1.** Let $n_1,n_2\ge1$ with $m=\operatorname{lcm}(n_1,n_2)\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, and let $r_1,r_2>0$ be arbitrary. Put $R = 1+\max(r_1,r_2)$ and $N = m\cdot\#\{t\in L:|t|\le2R\}$, which is finite by Lemma 2.2. Then:
1. every orbit of $G = GG_{n_1,n_2}(r_1,r_2)$ has at most $N$ points;
2. $G$ is finite, with $|G|\le\prod_{H\in\mathcal H_N}[F_2:H]$.

*Proof.*
1. Let $z\in\mathbb C$. If $z\notin U$, then $|Gz|=1$ by Lemma 3.2. Otherwise $|z|\le R$, because every point of $D_k$ satisfies $|z|\le|c_k|+r_k$.
2. By Lemma 3.2 every point of $Gz$ has the form $uz+t$ with $u\in\mu_m$, $t\in L$ (Lemma 2.1), and lies in $U$, so $|uz+t|\le R$. Then $|t|\le|uz+t|+|z|\le 2R$.
3. The map $(u,t)\mapsto uz+t$ from $\mu_m\times\{t\in L:|t|\le2R\}$ therefore covers $Gz$, so $|Gz|\le N$.
4. Proposition 4.2 with $X=\mathbb C$ and $k=2$ gives the second claim. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2.** $\operatorname{lcm}(n_1,n_2)\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$ holds exactly when $n_1,n_2$ both divide 4 or both divide 6. For all such pairs, and all radii, $GG_{n_1,n_2}(r_1,r_2)$ is finite. In particular $GG_{n}(r)$ is finite for every $r>0$ when $n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$.

## 6. Second proof: an invariant finite partition ("atoms")

This proof gives the structural picture the paper's figures suggest. Each element acts on each cell of one fixed finite partition of $U$ by a single element of $\Gamma$. It also settles the invariance question for the arrangement of circles. Assume $m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$ throughout this section.

**Definition 6.1.** A *circle* is $C(c,\rho)=\{|z-c|=\rho\}$ with $\rho>0$. Its *position function* is $\pi_C(z) = \operatorname{sign}(|z-c|-\rho)\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Let
$$\Sigma = \{\gamma(\partial D_1) : \gamma\in\Gamma\}\cup\{\gamma(\partial D_2):\gamma\in\Gamma\},$$
a set of circles. Note that $\gamma(C(c,\rho)) = C(\gamma c,\rho)$ since $\gamma$ is an isometry. Points $z,z'$ are *equivalent*, $z\sim z'$, if $\pi_C(z)=\pi_C(z')$ for every $C\in\Sigma$. The equivalence classes are the *atoms*.

Atoms need not be connected. Connectivity is never used.

**Lemma 6.2 (Γ permutes atoms).** For every $\gamma\in\Gamma$ and every atom $\alpha$, $\gamma(\alpha)$ is an atom.

*Proof.*
1. For an isometry $\gamma$, $\pi_{\gamma C}(\gamma z) = \pi_C(z)$.
2. $\Sigma$ is $\Gamma$-invariant, since $\gamma(\gamma'\partial D_k) = (\gamma\gamma')\partial D_k$, and $\gamma\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma$ is a bijection with inverse $\gamma^{-1}$.
3. Hence $\gamma z\sim\gamma z'$ iff $\pi_{C}(\gamma z) = \pi_C(\gamma z')$ for all $C\in\Sigma$, iff $\pi_{\gamma^{-1}C}(z)=\pi_{\gamma^{-1}C}(z')$ for all $C\in\Sigma$, iff $z\sim z'$.
4. So $\gamma$ maps the class of $z$ into the class of $\gamma z$, and $\gamma^{-1}$ maps it back, so the image is the whole class. $\square$

**Lemma 6.3 (atoms do not straddle the disks).** Each atom is contained in $D_k$ or disjoint from it ($k=1,2$). Hence $U$ is a union of atoms.

*Proof.* $\partial D_k\in\Sigma$ (take $\gamma=\mathrm{id}$), and $D_k=\{\pi_{\partial D_k}\le0\}$ is a union of classes. (For open disks, $\{\pi_{\partial D_k}<0\}$ is also a union of classes; the lemma holds for either convention.) $\square$

This answers the question about arcs crossing $\partial D_1$. A connected arc of some circle in $\Sigma$ may cross $\partial D_1$, and $a$ breaks it. But atoms are defined by position relative to *all* circles of $\Sigma$, including $\partial D_1$ itself, so no atom crosses $\partial D_1$, and $a$ acts on each atom either as $A$ or as the identity.

**Lemma 6.4 (finitely many atoms in $U$).** Only finitely many circles of $\Sigma$ meet $U$. If $M$ is their number, $U$ contains at most $2\cdot3^M$ atoms.

*Proof.*
1. *Finitely many circles.* The circles of $\Sigma$ are $C(\gamma(c_k),r_k)$. By Lemma 2.1, $\gamma(c_k)\in\gamma(L)=L$, since $c_k=\pm1\in L$. If such a circle meets $U\subseteq\{|z|\le R\}$, its centre satisfies $|\gamma(c_k)|\le R+\max(r_1,r_2)$. By Lemma 2.2 there are finitely many such centres, and with two possible radii, finitely many circles.
2. *Circles not meeting $U$ do not split a disk.* Let $K\in\{D_1,D_2\}$, a convex and hence connected set. If $C=C(c,\rho)\in\Sigma$ does not meet $K$, then $z\mapsto|z-c|-\rho$ is continuous and non-vanishing on $K$, hence of constant sign, so $\pi_C$ is constant on $K$.
3. *Counting.* Two points of the same disk $D_k$ with the same positions relative to the $M$ circles meeting $U$ are therefore equivalent. So each $D_k$ meets at most $3^M$ atoms, and by Lemma 6.3 every atom meeting $U$ lies in $U$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5.** Let $\mathcal P$ be the finite set of atoms contained in $U$.
1. For every $g\in G$ and $\alpha\in\mathcal P$ there is $\gamma_{g,\alpha}\in\Gamma$ with $g|_\alpha = \gamma_{g,\alpha}|_\alpha$, and $g(\alpha)\in\mathcal P$.
2. $g$ is determined by the family $(g|_\alpha)_{\alpha\in\mathcal P}$, and each $g|_\alpha$ is one of at most $N$ maps ($N$ as in Theorem 5.1).
3. Hence $|G|\le N^{|\mathcal P|}$, and $G$ acts on $\mathcal P$ by permutations, each atom carrying a "decoration" in the finite set $\mu_m\times\{t\in L:|t|\le2R\}$.

*Proof.*
1. *Generators.* Let $s = a^{\pm1}$ and $\alpha\in\mathcal P$. By Lemma 6.3, either $\alpha\subseteq D_1$ and $s|_\alpha = A^{\pm1}|_\alpha$, or $\alpha\cap D_1=\emptyset$ and $s|_\alpha=\mathrm{id}$. The same holds for $b^{\pm1}$ with $D_2$ and $B$.
2. *Induction on word length.* The case $g = \mathrm{id}$ is trivial. If $g = sg'$ with $g'|_\alpha = \gamma'|_\alpha$, then $\beta = g'(\alpha) = \gamma'(\alpha)$ is an atom (Lemma 6.2), and it lies in $U$ (Lemma 3.1). By step 1, $s|_\beta = S|_\beta$ with $S\in\{\mathrm{id},A^{\pm1},B^{\pm1}\}$. So $g|_\alpha = (S\gamma')|_\alpha$, and $g(\alpha) = S(\beta)$ is an atom in $U$.
3. *Finitely many restrictions.* Pick $p_\alpha\in\alpha$. Write $\gamma_{g,\alpha}(z) = uz+t$. Since $g(p_\alpha)\in U$, the argument of Theorem 5.1 gives $u\in\mu_m$ and $t\in L$ with $|t|\le 2R$. The restriction $g|_\alpha$ is determined by $(u,t)$. So it is one of at most $N$ maps.
4. *Determination.* $\mathcal P$ partitions $U$ (Lemma 6.3), and $g=\mathrm{id}$ off $U$ (Lemma 3.1). So $g$ is determined by its restrictions, giving $|G|\le N^{|\mathcal P|}$. $\square$

**Remark 6.6 (on the "finitely many pieces" step of the sketch in [`analytic-continuation.md`](../analytic-continuation.md) §1.3).** That sketch argues "piece boundaries are images of the two boundary circles, finitely many of which meet the bounded region, so there are finitely many pieces, and on each piece an element is one of finitely many $\gamma$". Two points need care, and Theorem 6.5 supplies both.
- That each element acts by a *single* $\gamma$ on each piece of a *fixed* partition requires the partition to be invariant (Lemma 6.2) and not to straddle $\partial D_k$ (Lemma 6.3). Neither is automatic for an arbitrary partition, and the sketch does not prove them.
- Taking the finite subarrangement $\Sigma_U$ (circles meeting $U$) as the definition also works, by the argument of Lemma 6.4(2). But it is not literally invariant: $A$ can map a circle of $\Sigma_U$ that meets only $D_2$ to a circle that misses $U$. Using the full locally finite arrangement $\Sigma$ avoids this.

## 7. The non-overlapping and tangent cases (all $n_1,n_2$)

These need no arithmetic.

**Proposition 7.1.** Let $n_1,n_2\ge1$ be arbitrary.
1. If $r_1+r_2<2$ (disjoint closed disks), then $G\cong C_{n_1}\times C_{n_2}$.
2. If $r_1+r_2=2$ (closed disks tangent at $x_0 = -1+r_1 = 1-r_2$), then $G$ is finite, with $|G|\le n_1 n_2\,(n_1+n_2-1)!$.

*Proof.*
1. $a$ and $b$ have disjoint supports, so they commute.
   - $a^{n_1} = \mathrm{id}$. For $1\le j<n_1$, $a^j$ moves every point of $D_1\setminus\{c_1\}$, a non-empty set since $r_1>0$. So $a$ has order exactly $n_1$, and $b$ has order exactly $n_2$.
   - The map $C_{n_1}\times C_{n_2}\to G$, $(j,k)\mapsto a^jb^k$, is a surjective homomorphism. It is injective: if $a^jb^k=\mathrm{id}$, restricting to $D_1$ (where $b$ is the identity) gives $a^j=\mathrm{id}$, and likewise $b^k = \mathrm{id}$.
2. If $n_1 = 1$ or $n_2=1$, the group is cyclic. Otherwise:
   - $D_1\cap D_2=\{x_0\}$. Let $O = \{A^jx_0\}_j\cup\{B^kx_0\}_k$. These points lie on $\partial D_1$ and $\partial D_2$ respectively, and the only common point is $x_0$, so $|O| = n_1+n_2-1$.
   - $O$ is invariant: $a$ permutes $\{A^jx_0\}$ and fixes $B^kx_0$ for $k\not\equiv0$, since these are not in $D_1$. The same holds for $b$.
   - Then $D_1\setminus O$ is $a$-invariant and fixed pointwise by $b$, since $(D_1\setminus O)\cap D_2=\emptyset$. Likewise $D_2\setminus O$ is $b$-invariant and fixed by $a$. Every point outside $U$ is fixed by both.
   - Restriction gives an injective homomorphism $G\to\mathrm{Sym}(O)\times\mathrm{Sym}(D_1\setminus O)\times\mathrm{Sym}(D_2\setminus O)$. Its image lies in $\mathrm{Sym}(O)\times\langle a|\rangle\times\langle b|\rangle$, of order at most $(n_1+n_2-1)!\,n_1n_2$. $\square$

*Closed versus open.* In part 2 closedness matters. With open disks the tangent case has disjoint supports and $G\cong C_{n_1}\times C_{n_2}$. With closed disks, $a$ and $b$ do not commute when $n_1,n_2\ge2$: $a(b(x_0)) = b(x_0)\ne a(x_0)=b(a(x_0))$.

## 8. Remarks

**Remark 8.1 (generality and boundary conventions).** The proofs in §§3–6 use only these facts:
- each $D_k$ is a closed or open round disk (§6 uses that it is bounded by a circle, to define $\Sigma$, and that it is connected, in Lemma 6.4(2));
- each generator is a bijection that agrees with a global rotation on its disk and is the identity off it;
- $U$ is bounded;
- translation parts lie in the discrete ring $L$.

So Theorem 5.1 holds for closed disks, for open disks, and for one of each, with no condition on overlap or on $r_1$ versus $r_2$. Arbitrary distinct centres reduce to $\pm1$ by a similarity of the plane.

**Remark 8.2 (the statement of Theorem 1).** As stated in the paper, Theorem 1 asserts an infinite member whenever $\operatorname{lcm}(n_1,n_2)\notin\{2,3,4,6\}$. This fails when $n_1=1$ or $n_2=1$. Then one generator is the identity, $G$ is cyclic of order $\max(n_1,n_2)$ at every radius, and yet $\operatorname{lcm}=n_2$ can be $5,7,\dots$; also $\operatorname{lcm}(1,1)=1$. The correct statement assumes $n_1,n_2\ge2$. With that assumption, the present note proves the "only if" half, and Proposition 2.4 identifies the condition with discreteness of $\Gamma$. The "if" half (infinite for some radius when $m\notin\{2,3,4,6\}$) is the paper's argument and is not re-checked here.

**Remark 8.3 (a general principle).** Theorem 5.1 is an instance of the following statement. *Let $\Gamma$ be a group of isometries whose orbits meet bounded sets in uniformly finitely many points, and let $G$ be a finitely generated group of bijections that are piecewise equal to elements of $\Gamma$ and fix the complement of a bounded invariant set. Then $G$ is finite.* Proposition 4.2 is what turns orbit bounds into group finiteness.

## 9. Computational sanity checks (not part of the proof)

These checks used exact rational arithmetic in $\mathbb Q(\mathrm i)$ and $\mathbb Q(e^{\mathrm i\pi/3})$. Disk membership was decided by comparing exact squared norms with rational squared radii, so boundary cases were decided exactly.
- Pairs $(n_1,n_2)\in\{(4,4),(2,4),(3,3),(6,6),(2,3),(3,6)\}$, equal squared radii $r^2\in\{1/4, 3/2, 2, 5/2, 4\}$.
- Starting points: all grid points of mesh $1/2$ and $1/3$ (in the lattice coordinates) lying in $U$.
- Every orbit was finite. The largest orbit sizes ranged from 3 to 38, well below $N$.
- At $r^2=1/4$ the group induced on the sampled orbits has order $n_1n_2$ (16, 8, 9, 36, 6, 18), as Proposition 7.1 predicts.

At larger radii the induced permutation groups are large (for example of order about $2.07\cdot10^{39}$ for $GG_6(2)$ on 205 points). These orders are only lower bounds for $|G|$, and they were not reproduced by the referee. The referee did reproduce the orbit counts (including the largest orbit, 38, for $(6,6)$ at $r=2$) and extended the orbit-bound check to twelve pairs of orders, unequal radii and boundary starting points, finding every orbit within the bound $N$. The scripts were scratch work and are not part of this repository.
