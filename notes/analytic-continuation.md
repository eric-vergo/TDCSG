# Two-disk compound symmetry as a model of analytic continuation

Authors: Eric Vergo and Claude (Anthropic).

This note explores research directions. It compares the two-disk systems GG_n(r) with analytic
continuation and with Hecke groups. Labels, notation and the closed forms of r_c² follow
[`overview.md`](overview.md); a proof labelled [proved] here is a sketch or short argument written
out in this note and not refereed.

Further notation:
- A(z) = ζ⁻¹(z + 1) − 1 and B(z) = ζ⁻¹(z − 1) + 1 are the *global* rotations about −1 and +1.
- a and b are their piecewise versions: A on the closed disk D_L(r), the identity elsewhere, and
  likewise for b.
- Γ_n = ⟨A, B⟩ ⊂ Isom⁺(ℂ) is the **global group**.
- O_r(x) is the orbit of x under GG_n(r).

---

## 0. Summary

1. **The global group is arithmetic** [classical]. Γ_n = T ⋊ ⟨A⟩ with T = 2(1 − ζ)ℤ[ζ_n], the
   translations by an ideal of the cyclotomic integers. Each GG_n(r) is a truncation of Γ_n to a
   bounded region, and as r → ∞ its action on any fixed compact set converges to that of Γ_n. In this
   precise sense **Γ_n is "GG_n continued to r = ∞"**.
2. **Orbit finiteness is a Galois-conjugate boundedness condition** [classical]. Through the Minkowski
   embedding of ℤ[ζ_n], an orbit is finite if and only if the Galois conjugates of the translation
   parts of its elements stay bounded. This has the same shape as **Takeuchi's arithmeticity
   criterion** for Fuchsian groups. It explains why the set {2, 3, 4, 6} in Theorem 1 is the same set
   that makes Hecke triangle groups arithmetic: both conditions say [ℚ(cos 2π/n) : ℚ] = 1.
3. **The "only if" half of Theorem 1** (the half the paper omits) follows from one principle
   [proved, sketch]: *a piecewise truncation of a discrete global group is finite*. Infinite two-disk
   groups come only from non-discrete global groups.
4. **A Cauchy–Hadamard analogue** [proved]. If x has an infinite orbit at r₀ but finite orbits at
   radii arbitrarily close below r₀, then the orbit at r₀ accumulates on a boundary circle *from
   inside*. This proves part of the paper's unproven assumption: some accumulation on the boundary is
   forced, though not at the specific point the closed-form radii use.
5. **The Hecke dictionary is exact at the level of groups** [classical]. Adjoining the half-turn
   R_π(z) = −z, which swaps the two disks, gives ⟨R_π, A⟩ = Γ_n ⋊ C₂. This is generated by rotations of
   order 2 and n, the same shape as the Hecke group G(λ_n) = ⟨S, U⟩ ≅ C₂ ∗ C_n.
   - For n = 3, 6 it is the **(2,3,6) Euclidean triangle group**; for n = 4 it is (2,4,4).
   - GG_n(r) is the analogue of the index-2 subgroup C_n ∗ C_n = ⟨U, SUS⟩.
   - The mechanism of non-discreteness matches: an element acting as an irrational rotation, which in
     Theorem 2 is a piecewise translation of a segment with ratio φ.
6. **The analytic version of compound symmetry recovers Hecke's dichotomy, and shows where the
   analogy breaks.** Consider Helmholtz eigenfunctions on D_L ∪ D_R that are a-invariant on D_L and
   b-invariant on D_R.
   - **Unique continuation** (the identity theorem) spreads the finitely many local symmetries into
     the non-discrete global group.
   - So for n ∉ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} no such eigenfunctions survive past an "analytic threshold" r_A. For
     n ∈ {3, 4, 6} a discrete torus spectrum survives at every r.
   - The finiteness of GG_n(r) on [1, r_c) is therefore a *non-analytic* phenomenon. Piecewise
     isometries break the identity theorem, and that is exactly what makes them "compound".
7. **Fractal and zeta-function side** [conjecture].
   - The self-similarity scale factors in the paper (φ for n = 5, √2 − 1 for n = 8, 2 − √3 for n = 12)
     are, up to inversion, **Pisot units of ℚ(ζ_n)⁺**, as in the Lowenstein–Vivaldi renormalization
     theory of piecewise rotations.
   - Prediction: unit rank 1 (n = 5, 8, 10, 12) gives "lattice" self-similarity. Unit rank ≥ 2
     (n = 7, 9, …) gives "non-lattice" behaviour, in the sense of Lapidus–van Frankenhuijsen. This is a
     candidate explanation for the **broken scale symmetry seen for n = 7**.
   - The natural Dirichlet series is an *orbit height zeta function* with non-negative coefficients.
     Landau's theorem applies to it, but trivially.

## 1. The global group, and GG_n(r) as its truncation

### 1.1 Structure of Γ_n [classical]

- A⁻¹B(z) = z + 2(ζ − 1). Conjugating the translation by t by A gives the translation by ζ⁻¹t.
- So the normal closure of A⁻¹B is the group of translations by T = 2(1 − ζ)ℤ[ζ], since μ_n spans
  ℤ[ζ] over ℤ.
- Since B = A·(A⁻¹B), we get Γ_n = T ⋊ ⟨A⟩, a "cyclotomic affine group".
- T has ℤ-rank φ(n), so it is discrete in ℂ if and only if φ(n) ≤ 2, i.e. n ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}.
  Otherwise it is dense.
- With unequal orders, ⟨rotation by 2π/n₁ about −1, rotation by 2π/n₂ about +1⟩ has linear part
  μ_lcm and a ℤ[ζ_lcm]-module of translations, discrete if and only if lcm(n₁, n₂) ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}.
  This is Theorem 1's condition.

**Minkowski picture.** Let j run over (ℤ/n)^×/{±1}, and let σ_j : ζ ↦ ζ^j be the complex embeddings.
The diagonal map t ↦ (σ_j(t))_j sends T onto a **lattice** in ℂ^{φ(n)/2}. The physical plane is the
coordinate j = 1; the other coordinates form the "internal space", as in cut-and-project
quasicrystals. Γ_n is non-discrete in the physical plane only because we look at one coordinate of a
discrete object. This is the Euclidean, abelian version of SL₂(𝒪_K) being discrete in ∏_v SL₂(K_v)
but not in a single factor.

**Galois symmetry.** σ_k(A) = A^k and σ_k(B) = B^k, so σ_k is an automorphism of Γ_n. On GG_n(r) the
corresponding map a ↦ a^k, b ↦ b^k is a surjective homomorphism from C_n ∗ C_n onto GG_n(r). Whether
it respects the relations of GG_n(r), i.e. whether it is an automorphism of GG_n(r), is **open and
computable** (§5, test 5).

### 1.2 Truncation and its limit [classical]

- GG_n(r) is a quotient of C_n ∗ C_n, as any group generated by two elements of order n is. Write N(r)
  for the kernel of the map onto GG_n(r).
- For r < 1, N(r) is the normal closure of [a, b].
- Neither GG_n(r) nor Γ_n is a quotient of the other in general. A piecewise element has different
  germs on different pieces, so there is no homomorphism GG_n(r) → Γ_n.
- What is true:
  - each g ∈ GG_n(r) has a **germ cocycle** c(g, x) ∈ Γ_n with c(gh, x) = c(g, hx) c(h, x);
  - for a fixed word w and a fixed compact K, once r is large enough the whole trajectory of every
    x ∈ K under w stays inside D_L ∩ D_R, and then c(w, x) = ρ_E(w), where ρ_E : C_n ∗ C_n → Γ_n is the
    global representation.
- So GG_n(r)|_K → Γ_n|_K as r → ∞, and any w lying in N(r) for all large r has ρ_E(w) = 1. This is the
  precise sense in which **Γ_n is the continuation of the family GG_n(r) to r = ∞**.

### 1.3 Finiteness from discreteness: the omitted half of Theorem 1 [proved, sketch]

*Claim.* If Γ_n is discrete (n ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6}, or lcm ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6}), then GG_n(r) is finite for
every r.

*Sketch.*
1. O_r(x) ⊂ Γ_n x ∩ (D_L ∪ D_R). A discrete group's orbit meets a bounded set in finitely many points.
2. Piece boundaries are images of the two boundary circles. These are circles of radius r centred at
   points of Γ_n{±1} in a bounded region, so there are finitely many, and they cut out finitely many
   pieces.
3. On each piece an element is one of the finitely many γ ∈ Γ_n that move that piece within the
   bounded region.
4. So there are finitely many elements. ∎

Step 2 needs care and is the step to check first. The discreteness argument of
[`arithmetic.md`](arithmetic.md) §0 gives the same conclusion from the lattice side; the written proof
in [`proofs/`](proofs/) is meant to merge the two.

The same argument works in any geometry: **a piecewise truncation of a discrete group is finite.** An
infinite compound group therefore certifies that its global group is not discrete. In a precise
sense, r_c measures how much non-discreteness a radius-r truncation can hide.

### 1.4 Orbit finiteness as bounded Galois conjugates [classical]

Take x generic, meaning not fixed by any non-trivial element of Γ_n. Then γ ↦ γx is injective. For
y = γx ∈ O_r(x) write γ(z) = ζ^k z + t_y.

- The physical coordinate t_y = y − ζ^k x is bounded, since y lies in the disks.
- Hence **O_r(x) is finite ⇔ the internal coordinates {σ_j(t_y) : y ∈ O_r(x), j ≠ ±1} are bounded.**
  A bounded subset of finitely many cosets of a lattice is finite.
- Equivalently, the "house" H(y) := 1 + max_{j≠±1} |σ_j(t_y)| stays bounded. An infinite orbit is one
  along which the dynamics produces translation parts of **unbounded height**. This is a
  Northcott/Kronecker-type reformulation.

Compare **Takeuchi's criterion** (1975; from memory, to be checked). A cofinite Fuchsian group Γ is
arithmetic if and only if:
- the field k = ℚ(tr Γ⁽²⁾) is a number field and the traces are algebraic integers;
- every non-identity embedding σ of k sends tr Γ⁽²⁾ to a bounded set.

For the Hecke triangle groups (2, q, ∞) the invariant trace field is ℚ(cos 2π/q) = ℚ(ζ_q)⁺. Takeuchi's
list gives arithmeticity exactly for q ∈ {3, 4, 6, ∞}.

| | two-disk GG_n | Hecke G(λ_q), q = n |
|---|---|---|
| field | ℚ(ζ_n), real subfield ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ | invariant trace field ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ |
| "always tame" iff | n ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6} (finite for all r) | n ∈ {3, 4, 6} (arithmetic) |
| reason | translation lattice discrete in ℂ ⇔ φ(n) ≤ 2 | conjugate traces bounded and trivial ⇔ ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ = ℚ |
| criterion shape | conjugates of translation parts bounded along an orbit | conjugates of traces bounded |

This is a **structural coincidence with a single cause**, [ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ : ℚ] = 1, not a deep identity. In
Euclidean geometry, discrete, crystallographic and arithmetic over ℚ coincide. In hyperbolic geometry,
non-arithmetic discrete groups exist (G(λ₅), G(λ₇), …) because there is more room.

## 2. Analytic continuation, made precise

### 2.1 Pseudogroups and germs: the right notion is Haefliger completeness

- Let P_r be the pseudogroup of local isometries of ℂ generated by A|_{int D_L} and B|_{int D_R}. Away
  from boundary conventions, its orbits are the orbits of GG_n(r).
- Germs of P_r are germs of elements of the global group Γ_n.
- Continuing an isometry germ is trivial, because an isometry germ determines a global isometry. So
  all the content sits in the **domains** on which germs are realized, not in the germs. This reverses
  classical continuation, where the germs are rich and the gluing rule ("agree on the overlap") is
  trivial.

The good dictionary is Haefliger's theory of pseudogroups of local isometries (with Salem and Molino)
[H85]:

| analytic continuation | pseudogroup of local isometries |
|---|---|
| power series with radius of convergence ρ(x) | germ [γ]_x ∈ P_r with the largest ball around x on which some element of P_r realizes it |
| uniform radius of convergence (no natural boundary) | **Haefliger completeness**: for x, y there are neighbourhoods U ∋ x, V ∋ y such that every germ from x to y extends to all of U |
| complete analytic function / Riemann surface | the closure P̄_r (complete case: a Lie pseudogroup; orbit closures given by a locally constant sheaf of Lie algebras, Molino's commuting sheaf) |
| natural boundary | the boundary circles, past which germs cannot be extended |

[classical] For a complete pseudogroup of local isometries, orbit closures are submanifolds, in fact
the leaves of a singular Riemannian foliation. So a **fractal orbit closure can only occur where
completeness fails**. §2.3 shows that completeness fails at the boundary circles. This gives a precise
reading of "the obstruction is a singularity on the boundary circle". Beyond r_c, in the interior of
the lens, P_r contains germs of a dense set of translations; orbit closures there are open sets,
consistent with Molino. The fractal at r_c sits between finite orbits (a proper, discrete regime) and
open closures (a complete regime).

**Quasi-analyticity, the sharpest point.** A pseudogroup is *quasi-analytic* if an element that is the
identity on an open subset of a connected domain is the identity on the whole domain. This is the
identity theorem, transplanted.
- P_r is quasi-analytic, since local isometries of a connected domain are rigid.
- **GG_n(r), as a group of global bijections, is not quasi-analytic**: a is the identity outside D_L
  and a rotation inside.
- So the passage from pseudogroup to compound group is exactly the passage from analytic to
  "bump-function" objects. GG_n(r) is to P_r as C^∞ functions are to analytic ones [classical]. §4.3
  shows that this is where the finite/infinite threshold r_c lives.

### 2.2 Local systems, monodromy, free products

- C_n ∗ C_n is the orbifold fundamental group of a sphere with two cone points of order n and one
  puncture, i.e. the (n, n, ∞) triangle group. It is the index-2 subgroup ⟨U, SUS⟩ of the Hecke group
  G(λ_n) = C₂ ∗ C_n [classical].
- Its representations into PSL₂(ℂ) with fixed rotation angles form a one-parameter family, the
  distance between the two centres in curvature units. The values are:
  - spherical (finite groups at special values);
  - **Euclidean ρ_E** (image Γ_n);
  - hyperbolic (discrete free product once the centres are far apart, by ping-pong; discrete at
    isolated closer distances, the triangle groups (n, n, m)).
- Euclidean geometry is the curvature-0 degeneration of this family [classical].
- **Monodromy proper is finite and uninteresting** [classical]. The isotropy germs at x (germs at x of
  elements fixing x) lie in Stab_{Γ_n}(x), which is cyclic of order dividing n. The "monodromy around
  the lens" is the commutator germ [A, B], a non-zero translation in T. It is a curvature-like
  quantity: it measures how far the two local symmetries are from commuting. But GG_n(r) is not the
  monodromy group of a local system on an r-dependent space, and N(r) does not depend on r through any
  topology.
- Verdict: this viewpoint gives the right **group-theoretic frame** (quotients of the Hecke-type group
  C_n ∗ C_n, and the Euclidean point of its character variety) but not a monodromy *mechanism*.

### 2.3 A Cauchy–Hadamard theorem for two-disk orbits [proved]

**Proposition.** Suppose O_{r₀}(x) is infinite and O_r(x) is finite for radii r arbitrarily close to
r₀ from below. Then
inf { r₀ − |y + 1| : y ∈ O_{r₀}(x) ∩ D_L(r₀) } ∪ { r₀ − |y − 1| : y ∈ O_{r₀}(x) ∩ D_R(r₀) } = 0.
In words, the orbit accumulates on (or meets) a boundary circle, approaching it from inside.

*Proof.*
1. Suppose the infimum is δ > 0, and take r ∈ (r₀ − δ, r₀].
2. For every y ∈ O_{r₀}(x), membership in D_L(r) is the same as membership in D_L(r₀): points inside
   stay inside, and points outside (|y + 1| > r₀ ≥ r) stay outside. The same holds for D_R.
3. Rotation about −1 preserves |y + 1|, so a_r^{±1} = a_{r₀}^{±1} on O_{r₀}(x), and likewise for b.
4. So O_{r₀}(x) is invariant under the radius-r generators and contains x, hence O_r(x) ⊆ O_{r₀}(x).
5. Building both orbits step by step from x uses identical moves, so O_r(x) = O_{r₀}(x) is infinite.
   This contradicts the hypothesis. ∎

Remarks.
- The set of radii at which an orbit is infinite is therefore an open set plus radii where the orbit
  touches the boundary. Combined with the paper's monotonicity of orbit sizes in r, it has the form
  (r*(x), ∞) or [r*(x), ∞).
- This is the analogue of **"every power series has a singularity on its circle of convergence"**. It
  turns half of the paper's unproven assumption (the limit point lies on the disk boundary at r_c)
  into a theorem: some accumulation point on the boundary exists. It does *not* show that the specific
  limit point of the paper's segment construction is on the boundary, which is what the closed-form
  radii need.
- The **Pringsheim/Landau refinement** would say *where* on the circle. For a series with
  non-negative coefficients the singularity is at the positive real point, because
  |Σ a_k z^k| ≤ Σ a_k |z|^k. The two-disk analogue would be accumulation at a canonical point of the
  circle, such as on the x-axis or at the lens corners. The paper's n = 8, 12 constructions approach
  the boundary along a path from a disk centre. No positivity principle that singles out the point is
  visible [analogy]. This is test 4 in §5.

### 2.4 Radius of convergence and divergence at r_c [conjecture]

- **Divergence law.** Let N_x(r) = |O_r(x)|, a non-decreasing step function. Conjecture: as
  r ↑ r*(x), N_x(r) ≍ (r* − r)^{−D} · P(log(r* − r)), with D the box dimension of the characteristic
  fractal and P log-periodic of period log(1/|u|), where u is the renormalization unit (§3).
  - Heuristic: at r* − ε, the self-similar cascade resolves down to depth k with |u|^k ≈ ε, and each
    level multiplies the count by about |u|^{−D}.
  - This is a pole of order D at r* in the "radius" variable. It is the direct analogue of
    |a_k|^{1/k} → 1/ρ.
- **Observed asymptotics of r_c(n)** [computed, from the paper's Appendix A]:
  - (r_c(n) − 1)·n² equals 39.13 at n = 50, 39.33 at n = 80 and 39.39 at n = 100. It approaches
    **4π² ≈ 39.48**.
  - Equivalently, (r_c − 1)/(2 − 2cos(π/n)) → 4; r_c(n) ≈ sec²(2π/n), with a difference of order
    n⁻⁴.
  - Compare 2 − λ_n ≈ π²/n² for the Hecke parameter λ_n = 2cos(π/n) approaching the parabolic
    threshold λ = 2.
  - Both are small-angle geometry: the lens height √(2(r − 1)) is comparable to the rotation step
    2π/n. This is **elementary, not deep**: the same power of n, for the same reason. The baseline
    g(n) of [`experiments.md`](experiments.md) makes it exact.

## 3. Fractals, Pisot units and complex dimensions

### 3.1 Renormalization units [conjecture, strong evidence]

The scale factors reported in the paper are:

| n | scale factor | field ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ | inverse is a Pisot unit? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1/φ | ℚ(√5) | φ: yes |
| 8 | √2 − 1 | ℚ(√2) | 1 + √2: yes |
| 12 | 2 − √3 | ℚ(√3) | 2 + √3: yes |

The critical radii satisfy r_c² ∈ ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ in every closed-form case: r_c(5)² = 3 + φ,
r_c(10)² = 4 − φ, r_c(8)² = 10 − 5√2 and r_c(12)² = 40 − 22√3.

This is the self-similarity mechanism in the Lowenstein–Vivaldi programme for piecewise rotations
over quadratic fields. There the renormalization constants are units of the field of definition, and
in the pentagonal cases they are quadratic Pisot units [LV].

Cut-and-project explanation ([classical] for the mechanism, [conjecture] for the fractal). Scaling
the physical plane by a real unit u with |u| < 1 scales internal coordinate j by σ_j(u). For the
scaled orbit to sit in the same "window", the internal coordinates must stay controlled, i.e.
|σ_j(u⁻¹)| < 1 for j ≠ ±1. That means u⁻¹ is a Pisot unit of ℚ(ζ_n)⁺. In the quadratic cases
|u|·|σ(u)| = 1, so physical contraction is exactly internal expansion. **Depth in the fractal equals
logarithmic height** of the orbit points (§1.4).

**Conjecture R.**
1. For every n ∉ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}, r_c(n)² ∈ ℚ(ζ_n)⁺. In particular r_c is algebraic, which answers the
   paper's question in conjectural form.
2. The characteristic fractal is invariant under a renormalization whose scaling factors are inverse
   Pisot units of ℚ(ζ_n)⁺.

**The n = 7 prediction.** The unit rank of ℚ(ζ_n)⁺ is φ(n)/2 − 1:
- rank 1 for n = 5, 8, 10, 12, the cases with closed forms;
- rank 2 for n = 7, 9, 14, 18, where no closed forms are known.

For n = 7 the cubic field ℚ(cos 2π/7) has the Pisot unit 1 + 2cos(2π/7) ≈ 2.247 (conjugates ≈ 0.555
and −0.802; norm −1), and other independent units. If the fractal's renormalization involves two
multiplicatively independent scalings, it is a **non-lattice** self-similar set in the sense of
Lapidus–van Frankenhuijsen. Such sets have no exact scale periodicity: patterns recur only
approximately and drift across scales. That is a candidate explanation for the paper's observation
that the n = 7 pattern "suddenly changes" after a zoom by about 5×10⁵ [conjecture; test 2].

### 3.2 A Dirichlet series attached to two-disk data

For generic x define the **orbit height zeta function** Z_{x,r}(s) = Σ_{y∈O_r(x)} H(y)^{−s}, with H
the house of §1.4.
- Its coefficients are counts, so they are non-negative [classical].
- A finite orbit gives a Dirichlet polynomial, which is entire [classical].
- For an infinite orbit, the counting function N(H) is finite, since orbit points lie in a bounded
  physical region and in a lattice. It is O(H^{φ(n)−2}), the real dimension of internal space. So the
  abscissa satisfies σ_c ≤ φ(n) − 2 [classical].
- By Landau's theorem, s = σ_c is a singularity [classical, but trivial].
- [conjecture] At r_c, σ_c = D (the fractal's box dimension, exactly in the rank-1 cases). The other
  poles D + 2πik/log θ (θ the Pisot unit) are the **complex dimensions** of the fractal: a lattice of
  poles on a vertical line in rank 1, quasi-periodic in rank ≥ 2.

This is the route to a *rigorous* bridge from these fractals to zeta-function language: the theory of
fractal strings and complex dimensions. In that theory Lapidus and Maier showed that the Riemann
hypothesis is equivalent to the solvability of an inverse spectral problem for fractal strings of
dimension other than 1/2 (from memory, to be checked); that is a reformulation, not a method of
proof.

## 4. The Hecke group / converse theorem comparison

### 4.1 Exact group dictionary [classical]

- Hecke: G(λ) = ⟨S : z ↦ −1/z, T_λ : z ↦ z + λ⟩. With U = ST_λ, tr U = λ. For 0 < λ < 2, U is elliptic
  with rotation angle 2 arccos(λ/2), of order q if and only if λ = λ_q = 2cos(π/q).
  - G(λ_q) = ⟨S, U⟩ ≅ C₂ ∗ C_q, the (2, q, ∞) triangle group.
  - It is generated by a rotation of order 2 about i and one of order q about p = Fix(U).
- Two-disk: R_π(z) = −z swaps the centres, and R_π A R_π⁻¹ = B.
  - Hence ⟨R_π, A⟩ = Γ_n ⋊ C₂ is generated by an order-2 rotation about 0 and an order-n rotation about
    −1.
  - **This is the Euclidean Hecke group.**
- C_n ∗ C_n = ⟨U, SUS⟩ ↔ ⟨A, R_π A R_π⟩ = ⟨A, B⟩ = Γ_n. The piecewise group GG_n(r) is normalized by
  R_π, and **GG_n(r) ⋊ C₂ is the piecewise Hecke group.**
- When the Euclidean Hecke group is discrete it is a Euclidean triangle group. R_π A is a rotation by
  π − 2π/n, so:
  - n = 3 or 6 give **(2,3,6)**, the wallpaper group p6;
  - n = 4 gives (2,4,4), the wallpaper group p4;
  - n = 2 gives p2.
- The hyperbolic arithmetic Hecke groups (2,3,∞), (2,4,∞), (2,6,∞) are the cusped relatives of these.
- (Hecke's T is parabolic; its Euclidean analogue is a translation. The pair ⟨half-turn, translation⟩
  is always a discrete frieze group, so the Euclidean analogue has to keep the (order 2, order n)
  generators, not (S, T).)
- [analogy] The (2,3,6) group is the symmetry group of the Eisenstein lattice ℤ[ζ₃]; ℂ/ℤ[ζ₃] is the CM
  elliptic curve with j = 0. In two-disk terms it belongs to the n = 3, 6 systems, which are finite at
  every r.

### 4.2 Mechanisms of non-discreteness match [classical, for the specific cases]

- Hecke: for 0 < λ < 2 with λ ≠ λ_q, U is an elliptic of **infinite order** (an irrational rotation),
  so G(λ) is not discrete.
- Two-disk, Theorem 2 (n = 5): at r_c, three words act on the segment C₀X as **piecewise translations
  with length ratio φ**, an irrational rotation of a segment (a 2- or 3-interval exchange).
- In both, the obstruction to discreteness or finiteness is an element that acts as an irrational
  rotation on an invariant set. This suggests sharpening the paper's open question ("does every
  infinite two-disk group contain a point with infinite image, or a generator of infinite order?")
  to: **at r_c, is there always an invariant segment or arc on which a word in GG_n acts as an
  irrational interval exchange whose lengths lie in ℚ(ζ_n)⁺?** [conjecture]

### 4.3 A converse-theorem analogue: compound-invariant eigenfunctions

The two halves of Hecke's correspondence are:
- T-invariance ⇔ a Fourier expansion, i.e. a Dirichlet series;
- S-invariance ⇔ the functional equation of its Mellin transform.

The two-disk analogue uses Bessel expansions about the two centres. For μ > 0, let E_r(μ) be the space
of solutions of Δf + μf = 0 on int(D_L ∪ D_R) with f∘a = f on D_L and f∘b = f on D_R.
- a-invariance ⇔ the expansion f = Σ_k c_k J_k(√μ ρ_L) e^{ikθ_L} about −1 is supported on k ∈ nℤ. This
  is the analogue of T-invariance.
- b-invariance ⇔ the re-expansion about +1, given by **Graf's addition theorem**
  d_m = Σ_k c_k J_{k−m}(2√μ) (up to sign conventions), is also supported on nℤ. This is a two-centre
  "functional equation", the analogue of S-invariance.

[classical, r = ∞] An entire eigenfunction invariant under A and B on all of ℂ is Γ_n-invariant,
hence T-periodic.
- For n ∉ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}, T is dense, so f is constant, so f = 0.
- For n ∈ {3, 4, 6}, the solutions are the eigenfunctions of the flat orbifold ℂ/Γ_n (spheres with cone
  points (3,3,3), (2,4,4), (2,3,6)). Their eigenvalues μ ∈ 4π²|T*|² form a discrete spectrum, with T*
  the dual lattice.
- **This is Hecke's dichotomy, transplanted**: non-trivial automorphic objects exist if and only if
  the group is discrete. (Hecke also showed that the functional equation has only trivial solutions
  for non-discrete G(λ), λ < 2; see Berndt–Knopp, *Hecke's Theory of Modular Forms and Dirichlet
  Series*. From memory, to be checked.)

[conjecture, with a sketch] **Analytic threshold r_A(n).**
- For r > 1 the domain int(D_L ∪ D_R) is connected and f is real-analytic.
- Suppose P_r contains a translation germ τ_t on an open set W. Then f(z + t) − f(z) solves Helmholtz
  on a connected domain and vanishes on W, so it vanishes on the whole connected component of its
  domain, by unique continuation.
- Once P_r contains translation germs t₁, …, t_{φ(n)} whose domains overlap in a connected way and
  whose ℤ-span is dense, f is invariant under a dense set of small translations near a point. Then
  ∇f = 0, so f = 0.
- So E_r(μ) = 0 for all μ once r > r_A(n). For n ∉ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} the paper's own bound gives
  r_A(n) ≤ 4.
- Restriction E_{r'}(μ) → E_r(μ) is injective for r < r' (unique continuation), so dim E_r(μ) is
  **non-increasing** in r. Orbit sizes are non-decreasing in r. The analytic and combinatorial
  monotonicities run in opposite directions.

**Why this matters.** One expects r_A(n) < r_c(n) in general. GG_n(r) can already be finite while
containing enough translation germs (pentagon-edge translations for n = 5 at r ≈ 2) for analytic
continuation to amplify them into a dense group. If so:
- **analytic objects feel the global, non-discrete group Γ_n early;**
- **piecewise objects feel only the truncation GG_n(r)** and stay finite until r_c;
- the interval (r_A, r_c) is where "compound symmetry" differs from "analytic continuation of
  symmetry". Its length measures the failure of the identity theorem for piecewise isometries.

This is the most concrete form of the guiding intuition. It is also where the intuition must be
corrected: finiteness of GG_n(r) **is not** the continuation of an analytic object; it is the absence
of quasi-analyticity.

### 4.4 Riemann's second proof as a two-domain gluing [analogy]

- **t-line.** Ξ(s) = ∫₀^∞ (θ(t) − 1)/2 · t^{s/2} dt/t is split at t = 1, the fixed point of t ↦ 1/t
  (the trace of S on the imaginary axis; i is S's fixed point). The two rapid-convergence regions
  {t > ε} and {t < 1/ε} overlap around t = 1 and are swapped by S. This is the analogue of D_L and D_R
  swapped by R_π, with the lens centred at the fixed point 0. Analytic continuation to all s works
  because the two regions **overlap**, the analogue of r > 1.
- **s-plane.** The half-planes Re s > 1 (Dirichlet series) and Re s < 0 (via the functional equation)
  do **not** overlap. The critical strip is the gap, and modularity of θ fills it. Under the
  dictionary s ↔ plane position, the critical line is the perpendicular bisector of the two centres
  (the R_π-symmetric axis), and Landau's real point is the line through the centres.
- This part is **numerology**. The two-disk system has no zeros, and its axes play no role analogous
  to Re s = 1/2.

## 5. What a theorem realizing the analogy would look like

**Theorem shape A (functor to Dirichlet series).** Send two-disk data (n, r, x) to Z_{x,r}(s) of §3.2.
- Wanted properties:
  - finite orbit ⇔ entire;
  - at r_c: abscissa = fractal dimension, with poles at the complex dimensions;
  - a Galois-equivariance under (ℤ/n)^×.
- Status: the definition and the elementary properties are [classical]; the dimension statements are
  [conjecture].
- This puts the two-disk systems inside fractal-string theory, not inside L-function theory.

**Theorem shape B (Hecke-type dichotomy for compound-invariant forms).** For each n and μ, describe the
non-increasing function r ↦ dim E_r(μ) of §4.3.
- Wanted results:
  - r_A(n) < ∞ for n ∉ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6};
  - E_r(μ) ≠ 0 exactly for μ in the torus spectrum when n ∈ {3, 4, 6};
  - r_A(n) < r_c(n).
- This would be a converse-theorem analogue: a two-centre Bessel functional equation ⇔ compound
  invariance.

**Theorem shape C (arithmetic of r_c).** r_c(n)² ∈ ℚ(ζ_n)⁺, with a renormalization by Pisot units,
proved through a Lowenstein–Vivaldi-style induction (first-return map) on the invariant segment of
Theorem 2.

**Theorem shape D (interpolation).** Hyperbolic two-disk systems HGG_n(r; d) in ℍ², with order-n
rotations about centres at distance d.
- At distances where ⟨A, B⟩ is discrete (free products, triangle groups), §1.3 gives finiteness for
  all r.
- [conjecture] r_c(d) → ∞ as d approaches a discreteness value, so that discreteness parameters act as
  "poles" of r_c.
- The Euclidean family is the curvature-0 limit.

### First steps and tests

1. **Exact-arithmetic orbit engine.**
   - Represent points by coordinates in ℤ[ζ_n]; decide disk membership exactly (sign of an element of
     ℤ[ζ_n]⁺, or interval arithmetic with exact fallback).
   - Record the internal coordinates σ_j(t_y).
   - Check §1.4: at r slightly below r_c the internal projection is bounded, and at r_c it is unbounded
     along the fractal.
   - Plot the internal-space "window" for n = 5; a Rauzy-type fractal window is expected.
2. **Renormalization units.**
   - For n = 5, 8, 12, confirm that the physical scaling by u matches the internal expansion by
     σ(u) = ±1/u.
   - For n = 7, 9, compute the units of ℚ(ζ_n)⁺. Test whether the n = 7 pattern change at a zoom of
     about 5×10⁵ matches a non-lattice combination of two independent Pisot scalings, or a switch
     between them.
3. **Critical exponent.** Measure N_x(r) for r ↑ r_c (n = 5, 8, 12). Fit (r_c − r)^{−D} with
   log-periodic corrections, and compare D with the box dimension of the fractal and with σ_c of
   Z_{x,r_c}.
4. **Where the orbit touches the boundary.** At r_c, for n = 5…20, record the points of ∂D_L, ∂D_R that
   the infinite orbit approaches (guaranteed to exist by §2.3). Test the Pringsheim-type guess that
   they are canonical points: on the x-axis, at the lens corners, or at the paper's constructed limit
   point.
5. **Galois action on relations.** For small n and several r < r_c, compute GG_n(r) as a permutation
   group on pieces. Test whether a ↦ a^k, b ↦ b^k (k ∈ (ℤ/n)^×) preserves the relations. If it fails
   for some r, that r is a "Galois-breaking radius"; compare it with r_c.
6. **Analytic threshold.** Compute dim E_r(μ) numerically with truncated Fourier–Bessel expansions and
   Graf's theorem (a finite linear system for the supports on nℤ). Locate r_A(n), compare it with
   r_c(n), and recover the torus spectrum for n = 3, 4, 6 as a check.
7. **Literature check before investing.** Haefliger 1985 and Álvarez López–Candel on completeness and
   quasi-analyticity; Lowenstein–Vivaldi on renormalization of piecewise rotations; Takeuchi 1977 on
   arithmetic triangle groups; Knapp 1968 and Gilman–Maskit on discreteness of two-generator groups;
   Lapidus–van Frankenhuijsen on complex dimensions.

## 6. Where the analogy breaks

1. **Germs are rigid.** Isometry germs continue trivially, so all the structure is in the domains.
   Analytic continuation has rich germs and trivial gluing; the two-disk system has the reverse.
2. **The compound group is not quasi-analytic.** Its defining feature (identity outside, rotation
   inside) is a failure of the identity theorem. Analytic objects built on it see the global group
   Γ_n, not GG_n(r) (§4.3).
3. **No monodromy representation.** Isotropy germs are finite cyclic. GG_n(r) is a quotient of
   C_n ∗ C_n, but the kernel N(r) depends on r through geometry, not topology. Neither GG_n(r) nor Γ_n
   is a quotient of the other.
4. **Landau's theorem is trivial here.** Orbit counts are non-negative and carry no sign. The
   two-disk system has no zeros, no cancellation and no explicit formula.
5. **Hecke has no r.** Hecke's dichotomy concerns the global group, and the two-disk finiteness
   question concerns truncations, a different question. The shared {2, 3, 4, 6} (via Takeuchi) has one
   elementary cause.
6. **Critical-line and axis matches are numerology.** The R_π axis has no reason to carry anything
   like zeros.

**Net assessment.** Worth pursuing as mathematics in its own right: the arithmetic reading of two-disk
systems (cyclotomic affine group, Minkowski / cut-and-project, Pisot renormalization, Galois
conjugates); the Hecke/Takeuchi comparison; and the analytic-versus-piecewise threshold r_A < r_c.
These are new questions about two-disk systems.
